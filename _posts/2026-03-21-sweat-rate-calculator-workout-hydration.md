Generic hydration advice can only take you so far.

One person may lose:

**0.4 litres of sweat per hour**

while another person training in the same room may lose:

**1.2 litres per hour**

or more.

That difference matters.

It means the same:

* bottle size
* drinking schedule
* electrolyte dose

cannot be assumed to work equally well for everyone.

If you want to make your exercise hydration more personal, one of the most useful measurements is your:

**whole-body sweat rate**

usually expressed as:

**litres per hour — L/h**

You can estimate it during an ordinary training session using:

* a scale
* your pre- and post-workout body weight
* the amount you drink
* session duration

This guide shows you how to calculate it and, just as importantly, how **not** to misuse the result.

For the broader hydration principles first, read [Hydration Basics: What Actually Matters](/hydration/hydration-basics-what-actually-matters/).

## Sweat rate calculator: the quick formula

For a normal session with no food and no bathroom break:

**Sweat loss (L) ≈ body weight before − body weight after + fluid consumed**

Then:

**Sweat rate (L/h) = sweat loss ÷ exercise duration in hours**

Because the density of water is close to 1 kg/L, an acute body-mass change of:

**1 kg ≈ 1 litre**

is a useful field approximation.

## Example

Body weight before training:

**80.0 kg**

Body weight after training:

**79.3 kg**

Fluid consumed:

**0.5 L**

Session duration:

**90 minutes = 1.5 hours**

Body-weight loss:

**80.0 − 79.3 = 0.7 kg**

Estimated sweat loss:

**0.7 + 0.5 = 1.2 L**

Estimated sweat rate:

**1.2 ÷ 1.5 = 0.8 L/h**

So under those conditions, your estimated whole-body sweat rate was:

**0.8 litres per hour**

That is the number you can use to make future hydration decisions more informed.

## The full formula

If you urinate during training or consume food, the calculation needs another step.

A more complete practical formula is:

**Sweat loss ≈ pre-exercise body mass − post-exercise body mass + drinks consumed + food consumed − urine produced**

Then:

**Sweat rate = sweat loss ÷ exercise duration**

For most gym or Muay Thai sessions:

* food consumed = 0
* urine produced = 0

so the shorter version is usually enough.

## Why body weight works

Over a relatively short exercise session, most rapid body-mass loss comes from fluid loss.

Research and sports-hydration guidelines therefore commonly use pre- and post-exercise body mass to estimate whole-body sweat loss. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

It is not a perfect laboratory measurement.

Small amounts of mass also change through:

* respiration
* substrate oxidation
* carbon dioxide production

These factors become more relevant during very long exercise.

But for normal field use, body-mass change corrected for drinking and urine is one of the most practical ways to estimate sweat rate.

## How to measure your sweat rate correctly

Small measurement errors can make the result much less useful.

Use the same method every time.

## Step 1: weigh yourself before training

Do it as close to the start of exercise as practical.

For best consistency:

* use the same scale
* use minimal clothing
* make sure clothing is dry
* record your weight accurately

Example:

**82.4 kg**

## Step 2: record everything you drink

Measure the amount in your bottle before and after training, or use a bottle with volume markings.

Example:

Bottle at start:

**1.0 L**

Bottle at finish:

**0.35 L**

Fluid consumed:

**0.65 L**

Do not estimate from memory if you want a useful result.

## Step 3: note any urine produced

If you use the toilet during the test, ideally measure or estimate the urine volume.

If you do not urinate:

**urine = 0**

## Step 4: weigh yourself immediately after training

Before weighing:

* towel excess sweat from your skin
* remove soaked clothing if practical
* use the same clothing conditions as before

A wet T-shirt can hold a surprising amount of fluid.

If you weigh yourself wearing soaked clothing, part of the sweat that left your body is still sitting on the scale with you.

That makes your apparent body-weight loss smaller and can underestimate sweat loss.

## Step 5: calculate total sweat loss

Example:

Before:

**82.4 kg**

After:

**81.6 kg**

Drank:

**0.55 L**

Urine:

**0 L**

Body-mass loss:

**82.4 − 81.6 = 0.8 kg**

Estimated sweat loss:

**0.8 + 0.55 = 1.35 L**

## Step 6: convert session time to hours

Examples:

| Session time | Hours |
|---|---:|
| 30 minutes | 0.50 h |
| 45 minutes | 0.75 h |
| 60 minutes | 1.00 h |
| 75 minutes | 1.25 h |
| 90 minutes | 1.50 h |
| 120 minutes | 2.00 h |

If the example session lasted 75 minutes:

**1.25 hours**

## Step 7: calculate sweat rate

Estimated sweat loss:

**1.35 L**

Duration:

**1.25 h**

Sweat rate:

**1.35 ÷ 1.25 = 1.08 L/h**

So your estimated sweat rate was approximately:

**1.1 L/hour**

## Sweat loss vs sweat rate

These two terms are easy to confuse.

### Sweat loss

The total amount lost during one session.

Example:

**1.35 L**

### Sweat rate

The amount lost per unit of time.

Example:

**1.08 L/h**

Sweat rate is usually more useful for planning because it allows you to compare sessions of different lengths.

## Example: same sweat loss, different sweat rate

### Athlete A

Sweat loss:

**1.0 L**

Session:

**60 minutes**

Sweat rate:

**1.0 L/h**

### Athlete B

Sweat loss:

**1.0 L**

Session:

**120 minutes**

Sweat rate:

**0.5 L/h**

Both lost one litre.

Their sweating rates were completely different.

## Your sweat rate is not one permanent number

This is extremely important.

You do not have one fixed sweat rate for life.

Your result can change with:

* temperature
* humidity
* exercise intensity
* session type
* clothing
* protective equipment
* airflow
* heat acclimation
* body size
* fitness
* individual physiology

Research in athletes shows large differences both **between people and within the same person under different conditions**. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

That means the best approach is not:

**“My sweat rate is 0.8 L/h forever.”**

It is:

**“My sweat rate was about 0.8 L/h during this type of session in these conditions.”**

## Test more than once

One measurement is useful.

Several representative measurements are better.

For example, you might test:

* normal gym session
* hard Muay Thai class
* sparring session
* summer training
* winter training
* long run

You may discover that your sweat rate looks like:

| Session | Sweat rate |
|---|---:|
| Cool gym strength session | 0.4 L/h |
| Normal Muay Thai | 0.8 L/h |
| Hard Muay Thai in summer | 1.2 L/h |
| Easy winter run | 0.5 L/h |

Now your hydration plan can change with the session instead of using one bottle size for everything.

## How should you use your sweat rate?

This is where people often make the next mistake.

They calculate:

**1.0 L/h**

and immediately assume:

**“I must drink exactly 1 litre every hour.”**

Not necessarily.

Sweat rate tells you how quickly fluid is leaving your body.

It does **not** automatically prescribe the exact amount that must enter your stomach during the same hour.

Your drinking plan also depends on:

* session duration
* starting hydration
* gastrointestinal tolerance
* opportunities to drink
* temperature
* whether you need rapid recovery afterwards

Sports-hydration guidance generally recommends individualising fluid intake so that dehydration does not become excessive while also avoiding overdrinking. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="3"}

## You do not always need to replace 100% during exercise

Imagine your sweat rate is:

**0.8 L/h**

and your workout lasts:

**45 minutes**

Estimated sweat loss if you drank nothing:

**0.6 L**

If you begin well hydrated and have access to fluid afterwards, there may be no practical reason to force exactly 600 mL during the workout.

Some of that deficit can be replaced during recovery.

Now imagine:

* the same athlete
* a three-hour event
* hot conditions

The same sweat rate would produce a much larger cumulative loss.

Fluid replacement during exercise now becomes more important.

The importance of drinking generally increases with:

**duration × sweat rate**

## Do not use a universal percentage

You may see rules such as:

**drink 60–80% of your sweat rate**

They can create a convenient starting estimate, but they are not a universal physiological requirement.

For example:

If your sweat rate is:

**1.2 L/h**

70% would be:

**0.84 L/h**

That may be reasonable for one athlete.

Another person may tolerate less.

Another may need a different approach because:

* the session is only 45 minutes
* the session lasts four hours
* drinking opportunities are limited
* conditions are extremely hot

Use your sweat rate to inform the decision.

Do not turn another percentage into the next generic hydration rule.

## A practical way to interpret your result

Think in terms of how large the deficit could become.

### Sweat rate around 0.3–0.5 L/h

During a short session, fluid needs during exercise may be modest.

### Around 0.5–1.0 L/h

Fluid replacement becomes increasingly relevant as the session gets longer or hotter.

### Above 1.0 L/h

Large cumulative deficits can develop quickly during prolonged exercise.

That does not automatically mean you must drink the full sweat rate.

It does mean you should pay closer attention to:

* fluid access
* drinking tolerance
* sodium losses
* recovery afterwards

These are not diagnostic categories.

They are simply a way to understand what the number means in practice.

## Gym example

Suppose your strength workout produces:

**0.45 L/h**

and you train for one hour.

If you started well hydrated, you may be perfectly comfortable drinking only when thirsty.

Now suppose a different gym is:

* hot
* poorly ventilated
* conditioning-heavy

and your measured rate is:

**0.95 L/h**

That is a different hydration problem even though both sessions happen in a gym.

Training location does not determine hydration needs.

Sweat loss does.

## Muay Thai example

Muay Thai can create substantial sweat losses because a session may include:

* skipping
* pads
* bag work
* clinch
* sparring
* conditioning

combined with:

* warm rooms
* protective equipment
* poor airflow

Suppose you measure:

Before:

**78.5 kg**

After:

**77.4 kg**

Fluid consumed:

**0.7 L**

Session:

**90 minutes**

Body-weight loss:

**1.1 kg**

Total estimated sweat loss:

**1.1 + 0.7 = 1.8 L**

Sweat rate:

**1.8 ÷ 1.5 = 1.2 L/h**

That tells you this is a high-loss session for you.

It does not mean you must suddenly try to drink 1.2 litres each hour in class.

Instead, it tells you:

* you are losing fluid quickly
* small occasional sips may leave a substantial deficit
* drinking opportunities matter
* post-session replacement matters
* sodium loss may also become meaningful

## Why big chugs can be a problem in Muay Thai

During striking, clinch and conditioning, a stomach full of fluid can feel awful.

Large rapid drinks may contribute to:

* sloshing
* fullness
* nausea
* discomfort during body movement

That is why practical Muay Thai hydration often works better with smaller drinking opportunities when available rather than trying to replace a huge deficit all at once.

If you consistently finish class with a large deficit, improve the whole strategy:

* arrive hydrated
* drink during available breaks
* continue rehydrating afterwards

Do not make one giant post-round drink do all the work.

## Running example

Suppose you measure:

Before:

**72.0 kg**

After:

**71.2 kg**

Fluid:

**0.3 L**

Duration:

**60 minutes**

Estimated sweat loss:

**0.8 + 0.3 = 1.1 L**

Sweat rate:

**1.1 L/h**

Now repeat the test during cool weather and get:

**0.55 L/h**

Neither measurement is wrong.

The environment changed your sweat requirement.

This is why sweat-rate testing is particularly useful for runners training across seasons.

## Hot-weather testing

If you compete or train in heat, try to measure sweat rate under conditions similar to those you actually care about.

A test performed:

* indoors
* in winter
* at easy intensity

may not tell you much about:

* outdoor summer running
* hot Muay Thai gyms
* competition conditions

Heat can substantially increase sweat rate.

Humidity can increase heat strain and change how effectively sweat evaporates.

For heat-specific guidance, read [Hydration in Hot Weather: How to Train in Heat Without Crashing](/hydration/hydration-in-hot-weather-training/).

## What about sodium?

Sweat rate tells you:

**how much sweat you lose.**

It does **not** tell you:

**how salty your sweat is.**

These are separate measurements.

Research shows substantial individual variation in both sweat rate and sweat sodium concentration. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

Two athletes can each sweat:

**1.0 L/h**

while losing very different amounts of sodium.

## Calculating sodium loss

If you genuinely know your sweat sodium concentration, the calculation is simple.

Suppose:

Sweat rate:

**1.0 L/h**

Sweat sodium concentration:

**800 mg/L**

Estimated sodium loss:

**1.0 × 800 = 800 mg sodium/hour**

Another athlete might have:

Sweat rate:

**1.0 L/h**

Sodium concentration:

**400 mg/L**

Estimated sodium loss:

**400 mg/hour**

Same fluid loss.

Half the sodium loss.

## Do not guess exact sodium loss from sweat rate

A common mistake is:

**“I sweat 1 L/h, therefore I need 500 mg sodium/h.”**

Sweat rate alone cannot tell you that.

You would need information about sweat sodium concentration.

Clues such as:

* visible salt marks
* salty-tasting sweat

may suggest meaningful salt loss, but they are not precise laboratory measurements.

For the full explanation, read [How Much Sodium Do You Lose in Sweat?](/hydration/how-much-sodium-do-you-lose-in-sweat/).

Also see [Sweat Rate vs Sodium Loss: What to Measure After Hard Training](/hydration/sweat-rate-vs-sodium-loss-after-training/).

## Do you need a sweat sodium test?

Most recreational athletes do not.

You can make a very good hydration plan without laboratory sweat testing.

A formal sodium test becomes more interesting when you:

* train for several hours
* compete in heat
* have very high sweat losses
* repeatedly need precise race hydration
* are an endurance athlete with large training volumes

If you do test, measurement quality matters.

Sweat sodium results can be affected by:

* body site
* collection technique
* skin contamination
* timing
* equipment
* laboratory method

That is one reason consumer sweat tests should not automatically be treated as perfect numbers.

## What should you do if you do not know sweat sodium?

Keep it practical.

If the session is:

* short
* cool
* only moderately sweaty

water and normal food may be enough.

If the session is:

* long
* hot
* very sweaty

then including sodium through:

* an electrolyte drink
* sports drink
* food

becomes more reasonable.

You do not need an exact sweat-sodium result before you are allowed to use electrolytes.

For the practical guide, read [Electrolytes for Workouts: When You Need Them](/hydration/electrolytes-for-workouts/).

## Do not drink more than you lose

This is the most important safety limit in the whole article.

Sweat-rate measurement is useful partly because it can show when your drinking plan is excessive.

If you estimate that you lose:

**0.6 L/h**

but routinely drink:

**1.2 L/h**

during prolonged exercise, you may be accumulating fluid rather than simply replacing it.

Exercise-associated hyponatremia is strongly associated with excessive fluid intake relative to losses during prolonged exercise. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="5"}

A useful check is:

**avoid gaining body weight because of drinking during exercise.**

If you finish prolonged exercise heavier than you started because of fluid intake, you have consumed more fluid than you lost.

## Electrolytes do not make overdrinking safe

This deserves its own section.

Someone may think:

**“My drink has sodium, so I can drink as much as I want.”**

No.

An electrolyte drink is still fluid.

You can still consume more water than your body is losing.

Sodium may be useful for replacing sweat losses.

It does not remove the risk created by large excess fluid intake.

Read [Can You Drink Too Much Water During Exercise?](/hydration/can-you-drink-too-much-water-hyponatremia/).

## What if you lose more than 2% of your body weight?

Traditional sports-hydration guidance often uses around **2% body-mass loss** as a point at which exercise-related dehydration may become large enough to affect performance under some conditions. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="6"}

You can calculate the percentage as:

**body-mass change ÷ starting body mass × 100**

Example:

Starting weight:

**80 kg**

Net body-mass loss:

**1.6 kg**

Calculation:

**1.6 ÷ 80 × 100 = 2%**

But do not treat 2% as a cliff edge where:

* 1.9% is always perfect
* 2.1% is always dangerous

Performance effects vary with:

* heat
* exercise type
* duration
* individual tolerance
* starting hydration

Use the number as context, not a diagnosis.

## How to calculate percentage body-weight loss

Use:

**% body-weight change = (pre-exercise weight − post-exercise weight) ÷ pre-exercise weight × 100**

This raw figure does **not** account for the fluid you drank.

It tells you the body-mass change observed on the scale.

For total sweat loss, still use the sweat-loss calculation.

## Post-workout rehydration

Your sweat-rate test also helps after training.

Suppose your calculated total sweat loss was:

**1.5 L**

and you drank:

**0.6 L**

during the session.

Your net body-water deficit may still be around:

**0.9 L**

assuming no other major losses or intake.

If you have the rest of the day to recover:

* drink normally
* eat
* include sodium through food if losses were substantial
* continue according to thirst

If another demanding session is coming soon, replacing the deficit becomes more urgent.

For the dedicated guide, read [How Much Water Should You Drink After Exercise?](/hydration/how-much-water-to-drink-after-exercise/).

## Two workouts in one day

Sweat-rate testing becomes especially valuable when you have:

* morning training
* afternoon or evening training

A large deficit from session one can carry into session two.

If you know session one typically costs you:

**1.4 L of sweat**

you can plan the recovery period far more intelligently than simply thinking:

**“I'll drink something later.”**

For the full plan, read [Two Workouts a Day: Simple Hydration and Recovery Plan](/recovery/recover-between-two-training-sessions-in-one-day/).

## Common mistake: weighing in wet clothes

This can underestimate sweat loss.

Dry yourself and standardise clothing.

## Common mistake: forgetting what you drank

If you lost:

**0.5 kg**

but also drank:

**1.0 L**

your sweat loss was not just 0.5 L.

It was approximately:

**1.5 L**

assuming no urine.

## Common mistake: forgetting a bathroom break

If you urinated:

**0.3 L**

during the session, that mass left the body but was not sweat.

It needs to be subtracted when calculating sweat loss.

## Common mistake: converting minutes incorrectly

75 minutes is not:

**0.75 hours**

It is:

**1.25 hours**

because:

**75 ÷ 60 = 1.25**

This simple mistake can significantly distort the final sweat-rate number.

## Common mistake: assuming one test applies to every workout

A winter gym test cannot automatically predict summer outdoor training.

Repeat tests under relevant conditions.

## Common mistake: replacing 100% by force

The calculator estimates loss.

It does not order you to put the exact same volume into your stomach during exercise.

Use the result with:

* session duration
* comfort
* recovery time
* environmental conditions

## Common mistake: using sweat rate to calculate sodium without sodium data

Sweat rate measures fluid loss.

Sweat sodium concentration measures sodium content.

You need both to calculate an individual sodium-loss rate.

## Common mistake: chasing perfectly stable body weight

A small fluid deficit during exercise can be normal.

The purpose of hydration planning is not necessarily to force body weight to remain exactly identical throughout every workout.

The goal is to avoid a deficit large enough to impair performance or health while also avoiding excessive fluid intake.

## How often should you retest?

There is no mandatory schedule.

Retest when something important changes, such as:

* season
* temperature
* session intensity
* duration
* training environment
* clothing
* body size
* heat acclimation

For many recreational athletes, a few representative tests are enough.

You do not need to weigh yourself around every workout forever.

## FAQ

### What is a normal sweat rate?

There is a very wide range.

Studies in athletes show substantial differences between sports, people and environmental conditions.

Do not judge your result only by whether it looks high or low compared with somebody else.

Use it to understand your own losses.

### Is 1 litre per hour a high sweat rate?

It represents a meaningful fluid loss, particularly during prolonged exercise.

But sweat rates around and above this level are seen in athletic populations, especially in hot conditions and demanding sports. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="7"}

### Can sweat rate be more than 2 litres per hour?

Yes, high rates can occur in some athletes and environments.

That does not mean everybody who sweats heavily reaches that level.

### Is 1 kg of weight loss exactly 1 litre of sweat?

Not exactly.

It is a practical approximation for acute field measurements.

Correct for what you drank and any urine produced.

### Should I drink exactly my sweat rate?

No.

Sweat rate estimates the rate of fluid loss.

Your actual drinking strategy should also consider exercise duration, starting hydration, tolerance and recovery time.

### Should I drink 60–80% of my sweat rate?

That can be used as a rough planning idea in some situations, but it is not a universal recommendation.

Do not treat it as a fixed rule.

### Can I use sweat rate to calculate electrolytes?

Not by itself.

You also need an estimate or measurement of sweat electrolyte concentration, particularly sodium.

### Does salty sweat mean I lose a lot of sodium?

It may suggest meaningful salt loss, but visual salt marks cannot provide an exact sodium concentration.

### How many times should I test?

Test enough representative sessions to understand how your losses change between the conditions you actually train in.

### Does my sweat rate change with heat?

Yes.

Environmental conditions are an important influence on sweating rate.

### Should I test during Muay Thai?

Yes, especially if you regularly finish sessions soaked or substantially lighter.

Just make sure you can weigh yourself consistently before and after.

### Can I calculate sweat rate during running?

Yes.

The same method works.

### What if I drink nothing during the session?

Then the formula becomes even simpler:

**Sweat loss ≈ pre-weight − post-weight**

assuming you did not urinate or eat during the session.

### Is sweat-rate testing medical advice?

No.

It is a practical way of estimating exercise fluid loss.

People with medical conditions affecting fluid or electrolyte balance should follow appropriate medical advice.

## Final takeaway

A sweat-rate calculation gives you something generic hydration advice cannot:

**your own measured exercise fluid loss.**

The basic calculation is:

**body-weight loss + fluid consumed − urine = estimated sweat loss**

then:

**sweat loss ÷ session duration = sweat rate in L/h**

Use that number to understand how quickly your deficit can develop.

Do not automatically drink 100% of it during every workout.

Do not infer your sodium loss from sweat rate alone.

And do not use the calculator as an excuse to force fluid beyond what you are losing.

Measure.

Compare different conditions.

Then build a hydration strategy around what your training actually costs you.
