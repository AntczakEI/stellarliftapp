Hydration around training sounds as though it should have one simple answer.

Drink this much before.

This much during.

And this much afterwards.

The problem is that two workouts can create completely different fluid losses.

Compare:

* 45 minutes of lifting in a cool gym
* 90 minutes of hard Muay Thai in a hot room
* a 30-minute easy run in winter
* a long run in summer
* two training sessions on the same day

The person doing the first session may lose very little fluid.

The person doing the second may finish with soaked clothes and a meaningful drop in body weight.

Giving both people the same bottle size makes little sense.

A better approach is:

**start reasonably hydrated, drink enough during exercise to avoid an unnecessarily large fluid deficit, do not overdrink, and replace meaningful losses afterwards.**

For ordinary recreational workouts, this can stay very simple.

For long, hot or extremely sweaty sessions, your own sweat rate becomes much more useful than a generic number.

If you want the bigger picture first, read [Hydration Basics: What Actually Matters](/hydration/hydration-basics-what-actually-matters/).

## The quick answer

For most normal training:

### Before

Drink normally during the day and arrive at training without obvious thirst or a known fluid deficit.

If you need a structured pre-exercise target, established sports-hydration guidance commonly uses around:

**5–7 mL of fluid per kg of body weight about 4 hours before exercise.**

For an 80 kg athlete, that is approximately:

**400–560 mL**

If you still appear underhydrated closer to training, an additional:

**3–5 mL/kg around 2 hours before**

may be appropriate.

### During

There is no universal hourly amount.

Drink according to:

* thirst
* sweat rate
* session duration
* heat
* access to fluid
* gastrointestinal comfort

For a short, low-sweat session, you may need very little.

For a long, hot, sweaty session, you may need substantially more.

### After

For an ordinary workout with plenty of recovery time:

**drink normally, eat a meal and continue according to thirst.**

After a large measured fluid loss — especially when another session is coming soon — replace the deficit more deliberately.

When rapid rehydration is necessary, sports-hydration guidance commonly recommends roughly:

**125–150% of the remaining fluid deficit over the recovery period**

with sodium included.

That does **not** mean you need to drink 1.5 times every small weight change after every workout.

## Hydration before exercise

The goal before training is not to fill yourself with as much water as possible.

It is simply to begin reasonably well hydrated.

If you eat and drink normally throughout the day and your last training session was many hours ago, you may already be fine.

You do not automatically need a special pre-workout hydration protocol.

A structured approach becomes more useful when:

* you trained earlier that day
* the weather is hot
* you have been travelling
* you have deliberately restricted fluid
* you woke up clearly thirsty
* you know you tend to start sessions dehydrated
* you are preparing for prolonged exercise

## The 5–7 mL/kg pre-workout guideline

One commonly used sports-hydration guideline is approximately:

**5–7 mL/kg around 4 hours before exercise.**

Examples:

| Body weight | Approximate fluid |
|---|---:|
| 60 kg | 300–420 mL |
| 70 kg | 350–490 mL |
| 80 kg | 400–560 mL |
| 90 kg | 450–630 mL |
| 100 kg | 500–700 mL |

This is useful as a structured starting point.

It is not something you need to calculate before every gym session for the rest of your life.

The advantage of drinking several hours before exercise is that you have time to:

* absorb the fluid
* produce urine if you drank more than needed
* make adjustments
* avoid starting exercise with a stomach full of water

## What if you still seem underhydrated?

Older but still widely used sports-hydration guidance suggests that if several hours later you still show signs consistent with inadequate hydration — for example, very little urine output or concentrated urine — an additional:

**3–5 mL/kg around 2 hours before exercise**

can be considered.

For an 80 kg athlete, that would be approximately:

**240–400 mL**

Again, this is a structured guideline rather than a mandatory ritual.

If you already feel normally hydrated, you do not need to keep adding fluid just because a formula allows it.

## Do not chug a litre five minutes before training

This is a common mistake.

You realise you barely drank all day.

Then five minutes before class you drink an enormous bottle.

That may give you:

* stomach sloshing
* bloating
* discomfort
* repeated bathroom trips

without magically fixing the rest of the day.

If you routinely start training dehydrated, fix the hours **before** the session.

Do not make the warm-up responsible for your entire daily hydration intake.

## What about morning workouts?

Morning training is slightly different because you have less time.

You have spent several hours asleep without drinking.

That does not mean you wake up dangerously dehydrated.

But if you wake thirsty, drinking before training makes sense.

A practical approach is:

* drink after waking
* give yourself as much time as your schedule allows
* avoid forcing a huge volume immediately before movement
* continue drinking during and after training according to the session

If your morning workout is short and cool, you may need very little.

If you are doing a long summer run or hard combat-sport session, the strategy deserves more thought.

## What if you use pre-workout?

Pre-workout supplements do not automatically create a special water requirement.

But they may contain caffeine and other ingredients, and people often take them immediately before training with very little fluid.

The bigger issue is still your **overall hydration status**.

If you want the specific guide, read [How Much Water to Drink Before a Workout if You Use Pre-Workout](/hydration/how-much-water-to-drink-before-a-workout-if-you-use-pre-workout/).

## Hydration during exercise

This is where universal advice becomes especially unreliable.

Your fluid requirement during exercise is mainly influenced by your sweat loss.

Sweat rate can vary enormously.

It changes with:

* body size
* genetics
* training intensity
* session duration
* fitness
* heat acclimation
* temperature
* humidity
* clothing
* airflow

Research has documented sweat rates ranging from very low values to more than 2 litres per hour in some conditions.

So a recommendation like:

**“Everyone should drink exactly 500 mL per hour.”**

will inevitably be wrong for a lot of people.

## For a short gym session

Imagine:

* 45 minutes
* air-conditioned gym
* strength training
* moderate sweating

You may only need a few drinks when you are thirsty.

Some people may barely drink during the workout and remain perfectly comfortable.

That is not automatically a hydration problem.

Training difficulty and sweat loss are not the same thing.

A brutal set of squats can feel harder than an easy run while producing much less total sweat.

## For a longer session

As session duration increases, fluid replacement becomes more relevant.

This is particularly true when you combine duration with:

* high temperature
* high humidity
* hard conditioning
* protective equipment
* limited airflow

Examples include:

* long runs
* cycling
* long Muay Thai sessions
* tournaments
* prolonged outdoor training

In those situations, having a planned drinking strategy becomes more useful.

## Drink according to the session, not the clock

You will often see advice such as:

* drink every 10 minutes
* drink every 15 minutes
* finish one bottle per hour

These can be useful reminders for people who consistently forget to drink.

But the clock does not know your sweat rate.

A better goal is:

**drink enough to limit an excessive fluid deficit without systematically drinking more than you are losing.**

## Do not try to gain weight from drinking during exercise

One useful safety check is body weight.

If you begin prolonged exercise at:

**80.0 kg**

and finish at:

**80.8 kg**

because you drank enormous amounts of fluid, you did not improve hydration.

You overconsumed fluid relative to your losses.

Excessive drinking during prolonged exercise is a major risk factor for **exercise-associated hyponatremia**.

That is one reason modern hydration guidance has moved away from rigid instructions to drink the maximum tolerable amount.

More water is not automatically safer.

For the full explanation, read [Can You Drink Too Much Water During Exercise?](/hydration/can-you-drink-too-much-water-hyponatremia/).

## How to calculate your sweat rate

If your training is very sweaty, this is one of the most useful things you can measure.

### Step 1

Weigh yourself before training.

Ideally use minimal or consistent clothing.

### Step 2

Record how much you drink during the session.

### Step 3

Weigh yourself again after training.

Towel off excess sweat first.

### Step 4

Account for any urine produced during the session if relevant.

A simplified calculation is:

**body-weight loss + fluid consumed − urine produced = estimated sweat loss**

Then:

**sweat loss ÷ exercise time = sweat rate**

## Example sweat-rate calculation

Before training:

**80.0 kg**

After training:

**79.3 kg**

Fluid consumed:

**0.5 L**

No urine during the session.

Body-weight change:

**0.7 kg**

Approximate total sweat loss:

**0.7 + 0.5 = 1.2 L**

Session duration:

**1.5 hours**

Estimated sweat rate:

**1.2 ÷ 1.5 = 0.8 L/hour**

Now you have useful personal information.

You do not necessarily need to drink exactly 0.8 L every hour.

But you now know that drinking only 100 mL during the same session in hot conditions will leave a much larger deficit than drinking 500–600 mL.

For the full method, read [How to Measure Your Sweat Rate Correctly](/hydration/how-to-measure-your-sweat-rate-correctly/).

You can also use [Sweat Rate Calculator: Your Workout Hydration Plan](/hydration/sweat-rate-calculator-workout-hydration/).

## Do you need to replace 100% of sweat during exercise?

Not necessarily.

Completely matching sweat loss during the session is not always:

* possible
* comfortable
* necessary

Someone sweating 1.5 L per hour may struggle to drink and tolerate that same amount while training.

The practical aim is usually to avoid allowing the deficit to become unnecessarily large.

You can then continue replacing fluid afterwards.

This is particularly relevant for long events where sweat loss may exceed what the stomach comfortably tolerates.

## What about thirst?

Thirst is useful.

For many recreational workouts, it may be all you need.

It is particularly useful as a safeguard against forced overdrinking.

But thirst is not the only tool available.

If you are:

* training for a long time
* competing in heat
* doing two sessions per day
* repeatedly finishing several kilograms lighter

then measuring your actual sweat loss can improve your strategy.

The most useful approach is not:

**thirst OR measurement**

It is:

**use thirst for everyday regulation and measurement when precision genuinely matters.**

## When do electrolytes become useful?

Water may be completely adequate during short or moderately sweaty sessions.

As sweat loss increases, sodium becomes more relevant.

You lose both:

* water
* sodium

through sweat.

Electrolyte replacement becomes more useful during:

* prolonged exercise
* very sweaty training
* hot-weather sessions
* repeated sessions
* situations where rapid recovery matters

Do not assume every bottle needs electrolytes.

But do not assume water is always the entire solution either.

Read [Electrolytes for Workouts: When You Need Them](/hydration/electrolytes-for-workouts/).

## Sweat rate and sodium loss are not the same thing

This matters if you are trying to become precise.

Sweat rate tells you:

**how much fluid you lose.**

Sweat sodium concentration tells you:

**how much sodium is present in that sweat.**

You can be:

* a high-volume sweater with moderate sodium concentration
* a lower-volume sweater with very salty sweat
* high in both
* low in both

Total sodium loss depends on both.

Read [Sweat Rate vs Sodium Loss: What to Measure After Hard Training](/hydration/sweat-rate-vs-sodium-loss-after-training/).

For sodium specifically, see [How Much Sodium Do You Lose in Sweat?](/hydration/how-much-sodium-do-you-lose-in-sweat/).

## Hydration after exercise

What happens after training depends largely on two things:

**how much fluid you lost**

and

**how quickly you need to recover.**

If you did a normal session and have the rest of the day available, recovery can be very simple.

Drink.

Eat.

Continue normal fluid intake.

You do not have to immediately replace every millilitre.

## Example: ordinary gym workout

You lift for 50 minutes.

The gym is cool.

You sweat modestly.

You finish slightly thirsty.

A perfectly reasonable recovery strategy is:

* drink some water
* have your next meal
* continue normal drinking

You do not need to weigh yourself, calculate litres or use an electrolyte powder.

## Example: hard Muay Thai session

Now imagine:

* 90 minutes
* warm gym
* skipping
* pads
* clinch
* sparring
* soaked clothing

You finish:

**0.8 kg lighter**

despite drinking during the class.

That is a much stronger reason to:

* replace fluid deliberately
* eat
* include sodium
* continue drinking across the recovery period

Same person.

Different session.

Different hydration requirement.

## How much should you drink after exercise?

If you have plenty of recovery time, normal food and fluid intake may restore balance naturally.

If recovery time is short, the strategy changes.

Sports-hydration guidance commonly recommends replacing around:

**100–150% of the remaining fluid deficit**

when rapid rehydration is needed.

The higher end is used because you continue losing some fluid through:

* urine
* ongoing sweating

during recovery.

A common practical target for rapid rehydration is around:

**1.25–1.5 L per kg of remaining body-weight deficit**

together with sodium.

## Example

You finish a very sweaty session:

**1.0 kg lighter**

and you have another hard workout in four hours.

A structured rapid-rehydration strategy may involve approximately:

**1.25–1.5 L**

spread through the recovery period, together with sodium and food.

That does not mean:

**drink 1.5 L in five minutes.**

Spread it out.

Use the recovery window.

## Why sodium matters after large losses

If you replace a large fluid deficit with plain water alone, some of that water may simply be lost rapidly through urine.

Sodium can help:

* replace sodium lost in sweat
* support fluid retention
* stimulate thirst

It can come from:

* a sports drink
* electrolyte drink
* salty food
* normal meals

You do not necessarily need a specialist hydration product.

Water plus food can work very well.

## When you have plenty of recovery time

This is an important distinction.

If your next hard session is tomorrow and you are eating and drinking normally, you usually do **not** need an aggressive 150% replacement protocol.

NATA guidance notes that with a longer recovery window, normal ad-libitum food and fluid intake can restore balance.

That is much closer to real life for most recreational athletes.

Do not turn every workout into a medical rehydration protocol.

For the dedicated post-exercise guide, read [How Much Water Should You Drink After Exercise?](/hydration/how-much-water-to-drink-after-exercise/).

## Muay Thai hydration

Muay Thai can generate substantial sweat loss because one session may combine:

* skipping
* technical drills
* pad work
* bag work
* clinch
* sparring
* conditioning

Many gyms are also:

* warm
* crowded
* poorly ventilated

That does not mean every Muay Thai student needs the same amount of water.

But if you train in those conditions regularly, learning your approximate sweat rate can be very useful.

## A simple Muay Thai setup

### Before

Arrive normally hydrated.

If you know you are behind on fluids, correct it in the hours before training rather than chugging immediately before class.

### During

Have fluid available.

Drink according to:

* thirst
* session duration
* how heavily you sweat
* temperature

### After

If the session was only moderately sweaty:

water and food may be enough.

If you are soaked and several hundred grams or more lighter:

replace the loss more deliberately.

Include sodium when sweat loss is large.

## What if you train Muay Thai and gym on the same day?

Now the recovery window matters much more.

The first session can leave you with deficits in:

* fluid
* sodium
* carbohydrate
* energy

If the second session begins only a few hours later, you have much less time to recover.

This is one of the best situations for measuring sweat loss rather than guessing.

For the complete plan, read [Two Workouts a Day: Simple Hydration and Recovery Plan](/recovery/recover-between-two-training-sessions-in-one-day/).

## Running hydration

Running deserves the same individual approach.

### Short easy run

You may not need to drink during it at all.

### Moderate run in cool weather

Drink according to thirst and practical access to fluid.

### Long run in heat

A planned strategy becomes much more useful.

Your sweat rate can also change dramatically between winter and summer.

Do not assume the number you measured in April is automatically correct for August.

## Hot-weather training

Heat changes the calculation.

Higher temperatures can substantially increase:

* sweat rate
* fluid requirement
* sodium loss
* physiological strain

Humidity can make heat stress worse because sweat evaporates less effectively.

In these conditions:

* start hydrated
* know where fluid is available
* consider your sweat losses
* include sodium where appropriate
* do not use hydration as an excuse to ignore dangerous heat

Water cannot make an unsafe environment safe.

Read [Hydration in Hot Weather: How to Train in Heat Without Crashing](/hydration/hydration-in-hot-weather-training/).

## Does urine colour help?

It can provide context.

If you wake up with very concentrated urine and are also:

* thirsty
* coming off a hard training day
* aware that you drank very little

then increasing fluid intake before the next session makes sense.

But urine colour is not a precise measurement.

It is influenced by:

* vitamins
* food
* supplements
* medication
* recent drinking
* time of day

Do not try to keep your urine completely clear at all times.

For the broader explanation, read [How to Tell If You’re Actually Dehydrated](/hydration/how-to-tell-if-you-are-dehydrated/).

## Signs that may fit with inadequate fluid replacement

After a sweaty session, you might notice:

* persistent thirst
* unusually concentrated urine
* low urine output
* dry mouth
* headache
* fatigue
* dizziness

These symptoms are not specific.

A headache does not prove dehydration.

Fatigue does not prove dehydration.

But if several appear after:

* heavy sweating
* heat exposure
* minimal drinking
* significant body-weight loss

then inadequate fluid replacement becomes a reasonable possibility.

For more detail, read [Signs of Dehydration During Exercise](/hydration/signs-of-dehydration-during-exercise/).

## Cramps do not prove you need more electrolytes

A cramp after training is not a sodium test.

Exercise-associated muscle cramps are multifactorial.

Possible contributors include:

* neuromuscular fatigue
* exercise intensity
* training history
* previous cramp history
* fluid and sodium loss in some circumstances

So if you had a long, sweaty session and lost substantial sodium, replacing that loss makes sense.

But:

**cramp = electrolyte deficiency**

is too simplistic.

## Common mistake: one number for every workout

This is probably the biggest mistake.

Someone decides:

**“I need one litre during every workout.”**

Then they drink the same amount during:

* 30 minutes of weights
* 90 minutes of Muay Thai
* running
* summer training
* winter training

Those sessions can have completely different sweat losses.

Use the session.

Not habit.

## Common mistake: chugging before training

Drinking a huge amount immediately before exercise can create discomfort without providing any special advantage.

Hydrate earlier.

Give the body time.

## Common mistake: refusing to drink during long exercise

There is no prize for finishing extremely dehydrated.

If your long training sessions repeatedly produce large body-weight losses, build a more deliberate plan.

## Common mistake: forcing fluid because somebody told you to

The opposite mistake can be more dangerous.

Do not keep drinking large amounts during prolonged exercise when:

* you are not thirsty
* your body weight is increasing
* you are consuming far more than you are losing

Overdrinking is not superior hydration.

## Common mistake: thinking electrolytes make unlimited drinking safe

They do not.

A sports drink still contains fluid.

You can still consume fluid faster than you lose it.

Sodium does not cancel the danger of overdrinking.

## Common mistake: waiting until bedtime to replace everything

If you finish a very sweaty afternoon session and remain thirsty for hours, there is little reason to wait until late evening and then drink two litres immediately before bed.

Spread recovery across the available time.

That is more comfortable and less likely to interrupt sleep.

## Common mistake: obsessing over tiny weight changes

Body weight naturally changes.

The sweat-rate method works best when measurements are:

* immediately before and after the same session
* made on the same scale
* under comparable clothing conditions

Do not interpret ordinary day-to-day weight fluctuation as litres of dehydration.

## A simple decision tree

### Before training

Are you normally hydrated and drinking normally?

**Yes:** carry on.

**No:** begin drinking in the hours before exercise.

### During training

Short, cool and low-sweat?

**Drink as needed.**

Long, hot or very sweaty?

**Use thirst plus your known sweat rate to build a more deliberate strategy.**

### After training

Small loss and plenty of time before your next session?

**Drink and eat normally.**

Large measured loss or another session soon?

**Replace fluid and sodium more deliberately.**

That covers most situations.

## Example 1: 45-minute gym workout

Conditions:

* indoor
* cool
* modest sweating
* no second session

Plan:

### Before

Normal drinking.

### During

A few drinks if thirsty.

### After

Water with your meal.

No complex calculation required.

## Example 2: 90-minute Muay Thai session

Conditions:

* warm gym
* hard pads
* clinch
* sparring
* heavy sweating

Plan:

### Before

Start hydrated.

### During

Drink regularly according to thirst and known sweat rate.

### After

Replace meaningful fluid loss.

Include sodium through food or drink.

## Example 3: long summer run

Conditions:

* prolonged exercise
* high temperature
* high sweat loss

Plan:

### Before

Pay more attention to starting hydration.

### During

Use a planned drinking strategy informed by prior sweat-rate measurements.

Consider sodium.

### After

Measure or estimate the remaining deficit and replace it across recovery.

## Example 4: two sessions in one day

Morning:

hard Muay Thai.

Evening:

gym.

This is where post-session hydration becomes more urgent.

After the morning session:

* measure loss if practical
* replace fluid
* include sodium
* eat
* continue drinking across the afternoon

Do not wait until five minutes before the second workout.

## FAQ

### How much water should I drink before a workout?

If you already drink normally and are well hydrated, you may not need a special amount.

When a structured approach is useful, approximately **5–7 mL/kg around four hours before exercise** is a commonly used sports-hydration guideline.

### How much water should I drink 30 minutes before training?

There is no universal 30-minute target.

If you are already hydrated, drink according to thirst.

Avoid forcing a very large volume immediately before exercise.

### How much water should I drink during the gym?

For a short session with modest sweating, drink according to thirst.

Longer or hotter sessions may require more.

### How much should I drink per hour while exercising?

There is no single correct amount.

Use your sweat rate, thirst, environment and tolerance.

### Should I drink every 15 minutes?

You can if regular drinking helps you manage a long or sweaty session, but there is no need for everyone to follow an exact 15-minute schedule.

### Should I drink during a 45-minute workout?

If you are thirsty, yes.

If conditions are cool and you barely sweat, you may need very little.

### Do I need electrolytes during a one-hour workout?

Usually not automatically.

They become more useful as sweat and sodium losses increase.

### How much should I drink after a workout?

For ordinary recovery, drink and eat normally.

After a large fluid deficit with a short recovery window, around **125–150% of the remaining deficit** may be used to support more complete rehydration.

### If I lose 1 kg during exercise, did I lose 1 litre of sweat?

Approximately 1 kg of acute body-weight loss represents about 1 litre of net fluid loss.

To estimate total sweat loss, also account for what you drank and any urine produced during exercise.

### Should I replace all sweat during training?

Not necessarily.

Some deficit can be replaced afterwards.

The goal is to avoid excessive dehydration without overdrinking.

### Does being thirsty mean I already waited too long?

Not necessarily.

Thirst is a normal fluid-regulation signal and can be useful for everyday exercise hydration.

### Can I drink too much during exercise?

Yes.

Excessive fluid intake during prolonged exercise can contribute to exercise-associated hyponatremia.

### Should I drink more if my urine is dark?

Concentrated urine can be one clue that you may need more fluid, but urine colour should be interpreted with thirst, recent drinking and the broader context.

## Final takeaway

Hydration around training does not require one magic bottle size.

Before exercise:

**start reasonably hydrated.**

During exercise:

**drink according to the demands of the session, your thirst and your sweat rate — without forcing excessive fluid.**

After exercise:

**replace meaningful losses, and become more deliberate when recovery time is short.**

A short gym workout and a long, sweaty Muay Thai session are not the same hydration problem.

The more demanding the conditions become, the more useful it is to stop guessing and measure what you actually lose.
