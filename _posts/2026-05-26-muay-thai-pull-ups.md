Pull-ups are one of the simplest ways to build and measure upper-body pulling strength.

You need:

* a bar
* your bodyweight
* a progression method

and not much else.

That makes them attractive for Muay Thai fighters.

But the important question is not:

**“Are pull-ups a good exercise?”**

They clearly can be.

The better question is:

**“What do pull-ups actually add to Muay Thai training?”**

The answer is more limited than some fighter programs suggest.

Pull-ups can build:

* vertical pulling strength
* lat strength
* elbow-flexor strength
* grip demand
* upper-back strength
* general upper-body capacity

Those qualities may be useful around:

* clinching
* hand fighting
* posture control
* pulling
* general strength training

But pull-ups do not directly teach:

* clinch technique
* punching mechanics
* defensive positioning
* timing
* balance against an opponent

And there is no good evidence showing that:

**more pull-ups automatically make you better at Muay Thai.**

They are a support exercise.

That is enough reason to use them.

## The short answer

Yes:

**pull-ups can be useful for Muay Thai fighters.**

They are especially useful if you want a simple way to train:

* lats
* upper back
* biceps
* forearms
* grip
* vertical pulling strength

But you do not need:

* huge pull-up volume
* daily pull-ups
* extreme weighted pull-ups
* 20 different grip variations

for them to work.

A fighter usually needs:

**enough pulling strength to support the wider program without making Muay Thai worse.**

## What muscles do pull-ups train?

Pull-ups involve multiple muscles around the:

* shoulder
* upper back
* arm
* forearm
* trunk

Important contributors include:

* latissimus dorsi
* biceps brachii
* brachioradialis
* trapezius
* rotator-cuff musculature
* pectoral musculature to some degree
* forearm and gripping muscles

EMG studies comparing pull-up and chin-up variations show substantial activity across several of these muscle groups.

But EMG does not tell us:

**how much a pull-up will improve Muay Thai performance.**

It simply helps describe which muscles are active during the exercise.

For a wider look at muscular demands in the sport, read [What Muscles Does Muay Thai Work? Full-Body Breakdown for Fighters](/training/what-muscles-does-muay-thai-work/).

## Pull-ups train vertical pulling

This is the simplest way to understand their role.

### Pull-up

Primarily:

**vertical pull**

### Row

Primarily:

**horizontal pull**

Both can be useful.

They are not interchangeable, and one is not automatically:

**more functional for fighters.**

A compact upper-body program may use:

* one vertical pull
* one horizontal pull

rather than trying to choose a winner.

## Do pull-ups help the clinch?

Potentially as general strength support.

But this needs careful wording.

The clinch involves much more than:

**pulling strength.**

It also requires:

* position
* timing
* balance
* posture
* hand fighting
* framing
* head position
* sensitivity to an opponent
* repeated isometric and dynamic effort

Pull-ups do not reproduce those demands.

There are no direct intervention studies showing that increasing pull-up strength produces a specific improvement in:

**Muay Thai clinch performance.**

So the most accurate conclusion is:

**pull-ups can strengthen muscles and pulling qualities that may support clinch work, but clinch ability itself must be developed through clinch training.**

## Strength supports skill

This distinction applies throughout combat sports.

A systematic review of strength training in Olympic combat-sport athletes found improvements in qualities including:

* maximal strength
* isometric strength
* muscular power
* balance

and some sport-specific outcomes.

That supports strength training as part of combat-sport preparation.

But it does not mean every gym exercise has a direct one-to-one transfer to one fight technique.

A pull-up can make you:

**better at producing pulling force.**

Muay Thai training teaches you:

**when and how to use your body against an opponent.**

## Pull-ups and grip

Pull-ups also challenge your ability to hold onto the bar.

That gives them a grip component.

Grip matters during:

* clinch
* hand fighting
* controlling arms
* general contact work

But again:

**bar grip is not clinch grip.**

The hand positions, forces and tactical demands are different.

So pull-ups can build general grip capacity.

They do not replace:

* actual clinch rounds.

## Pull-ups and posture

The muscles trained in pull-ups contribute to upper-body strength and shoulder movement.

That may support your general ability to maintain position under load.

But avoid claims like:

**“pull-ups fix fighter posture.”**

Posture during fighting is task-specific.

Your Muay Thai stance and clinch position are technical skills.

The gym can make you stronger.

It cannot prescribe one universally correct posture.

---

## Pull-ups vs rows for Muay Thai

You do not need to choose between them.

A simple strength program can include both.

### Pull-ups or pulldowns

Train vertical pulling.

### Rows

Train horizontal pulling.

That creates a broader upper-body pulling stimulus.

## Are rows more important for shoulder health?

Be cautious with that claim.

You will often hear:

**“fighters punch a lot, so they need twice as much pulling as pressing.”**

There is no established universal:

**2:1 pulling-to-pushing ratio**

for Muay Thai fighters.

Rows are useful.

Pull-ups are useful.

Presses can also be useful.

Program enough of each to build strength without excessive fatigue.

# Pull-up grip variations

You do not need to obsess over grip selection.

Research comparing:

* pronated pull-ups
* supinated chin-ups
* neutral-grip pull-ups
* rope pull-ups

has found many similarities in overall upper-body muscle activation, with some differences between individual muscles and phases.

That means:

**grip matters somewhat, but there is no universally superior fighter grip.**

Choose a variation you can:

* perform comfortably
* progressively overload
* recover from

## 1. Pronated pull-up

Palms face away from you.

This is the conventional pull-up.

Use a grip around:

* shoulder width
* slightly wider

according to comfort.

You do not need an extreme wide grip.

## 2. Neutral-grip pull-up

Palms face each other.

Some people simply prefer this position.

If it feels comfortable on your:

* wrists
* elbows
* shoulders

it is an excellent choice.

Do not call it automatically:

**safer**

for every athlete.

Individual tolerance varies.

## 3. Chin-up

Palms face toward you.

Chin-ups typically involve substantial elbow-flexor contribution.

Research comparing chin-ups and pull-ups has found some differences in muscle activation, including greater biceps activity in chin-up conditions in some studies.

That does not make chin-ups:

* easier for everyone
* better for fighters

They are simply another vertical pulling variation.

## Which grip should a fighter use?

Use the one you can train consistently.

You can even rotate between:

* neutral
* pronated

across training blocks.

There is no need to change grips every workout.

# Assisted pull-ups

If you cannot yet perform clean bodyweight pull-ups:

use assistance.

Good options include:

* assisted pull-up machine
* resistance band
* controlled foot support
* pulldown machine

There is no reason to perform:

* swinging
* half-rep
* uncontrolled

pull-ups simply to avoid assistance.

## Assisted pull-up prescription

Start with:

**3 sets × 5–8 reps**

using enough assistance that most sets finish with approximately:

**2–3 reps in reserve.**

When you can perform:

**3 × 8**

comfortably:

reduce the assistance slightly.

Then rebuild.

## Machine assistance

An assisted pull-up machine is particularly easy to progress because you can:

* reduce assistance gradually
* repeat the same setup
* track progress

Use it if your gym has one.

## Resistance bands

Bands also work.

The limitation is that assistance changes throughout the movement.

A band helps more where it is stretched more.

That is not necessarily a problem.

Just keep:

* the same band
* similar setup

when tracking progress.

---

# Negative pull-ups

Eccentric or negative pull-ups can help someone build toward the full movement.

Start at the top.

Then lower under control.

A practical approach:

**2–3 sets × 3–5 reps**

with roughly:

**3–5 seconds**

of controlled lowering.

## Do not overdo negatives

Eccentric training can create substantial soreness when it is:

* new
* high volume

So do not perform:

**30 negatives**

because you cannot yet perform normal pull-ups.

Use a small amount.

Combine them with:

* assisted pull-ups
* pulldowns

if needed.

---

# Bodyweight pull-ups

Once you can perform normal pull-ups:

you can use a rep range rather than constantly testing your maximum.

A useful starting structure:

**3 sets × 4–8 reps**

with approximately:

**1–3 RIR.**

## Example

### Week 1

6 / 5 / 4

### Week 2

6 / 6 / 5

### Week 3

7 / 6 / 6

### Week 4

8 / 7 / 6

### Week 5

8 / 8 / 8

Now you can decide whether to:

* add a small load
* continue building reps

depending on your goals.

---

# Weighted pull-ups

Weighted pull-ups are useful when bodyweight repetitions have become relatively easy and you want to continue developing:

**vertical pulling strength.**

You do not need them simply because:

**fighters should be explosive and strong.**

They are one progression option.

## When to add weight

A reasonable point is when you can perform something like:

**8–10 clean bodyweight repetitions**

without reaching failure.

That is not a scientific threshold.

It is just a practical point where adding resistance often becomes easier than chasing:

**15, 20 or 25 reps.**

## Weighted pull-up prescription

A simple strength-focused range is:

**3–4 sets × 3–6 reps**

at around:

**1–3 RIR.**

Rest:

**2–4 minutes**

when the sets are demanding.

## Start small

Add:

* 2.5 kg
* 5 kg

rather than immediately hanging:

**20 kg**

from your waist.

Progress slowly.

The exercise already includes your:

* entire bodyweight.

A small external load can be a meaningful percentage increase.

---

# You do not need maximum weighted pull-ups

If Muay Thai is the priority:

there is little reason to specialise in:

**1RM weighted pull-ups**

unless that is something you personally enjoy.

Your goal is supportive strength.

Not becoming a weighted-calisthenics competitor.

If progressing weighted pull-ups requires:

* more sessions
* more recovery
* more elbow stress
* reduced Muay Thai quality

the trade-off may no longer make sense.

---

# How many pull-ups should fighters do?

There is no fighter-specific research establishing an exact volume.

So use a practical amount.

## Beginner

Assisted pull-up:

**2–3 sets × 5–8**

once or twice per week.

## Intermediate

Bodyweight pull-up:

**3 sets × 4–8**

once or twice per week.

## Stronger athlete

Weighted pull-up:

**3–4 sets × 3–6**

once or twice per week.

These are starting templates.

Not physiological rules.

## You probably do not need high-volume pull-up challenges

Examples:

* 100 pull-ups
* daily pull-ups
* maximum reps every session

may improve your ability to:

**do lots of pull-ups.**

But that does not mean they are the best use of recovery capacity for Muay Thai.

---

# How hard should pull-up sets be?

Most of the time:

stop before failure.

A practical target is:

**1–3 repetitions in reserve.**

That lets you:

* maintain cleaner reps
* accumulate useful work
* manage fatigue

## Why not failure every set?

Resistance training performed to failure creates greater acute fatigue than non-failure training.

A systematic review and meta-analysis found greater:

* loss of biomechanical performance
* metabolic stress
* perceived exertion
* muscle-damage response

after failure training.

That does not make failure:

**bad.**

It means it has a cost.

For a Muay Thai athlete who also needs to:

* punch
* clinch
* hold pads
* grip
* spar

that cost matters.

## Can you occasionally go to failure?

Yes.

A final bodyweight pull-up set taken to technical failure occasionally is not automatically a problem.

But it does not need to be:

**your default training method.**

# How to progress pull-ups

Use the same progression principles as any other resistance exercise.

You can progress through:

* less assistance
* more repetitions
* additional load
* cleaner full-range repetitions

## Assisted progression

Example:

### Week 1

35 kg assistance  
8 / 7 / 6

### Week 2

35 kg  
8 / 8 / 7

### Week 3

35 kg  
8 / 8 / 8

Then reduce assistance:

### Week 4

30 kg  
7 / 6 / 5

Build again.

## Bodyweight progression

Target:

**3 × 5–8**

Build from:

**6 / 5 / 5**

toward:

**8 / 8 / 8.**

Then consider adding load.

## Weighted progression

Target:

**3 × 4–6**

Example:

### 5 kg

6 / 6 / 6

Then:

### 7.5 kg

5 / 5 / 4

Build again.

That is simple progressive overload.

# What counts as a clean pull-up?

You do not need to turn pull-ups into a gymnastics competition.

But comparisons should be reasonably consistent.

A useful rep generally includes:

* controlled start
* deliberate pull
* chin reaching bar level or above
* controlled descent

The exact range can vary with:

* anatomy
* equipment
* comfort

The main point is:

**do not progressively shorten the rep just to record more repetitions.**

## Dead hang every repetition?

You do not necessarily need an exaggerated passive:

**shoulders-to-ears dead hang**

before every rep.

Some athletes prefer maintaining some active shoulder tension.

Use a comfortable controlled bottom position.

Avoid:

* bouncing
* sudden jerking

simply to create momentum.

---

# What about kipping pull-ups?

Kipping pull-ups are a different skill.

They use substantial:

* body swing
* momentum
* coordinated hip movement

That makes them useful in sports where:

**kipping pull-ups themselves are a performance task.**

For Muay Thai strength training:

strict or controlled pull-ups are easier to use as a measurable vertical-pulling exercise.

There is usually no need to learn kipping purely for fight preparation.

---

# Common mistake #1 — swinging every rep

If your goal is vertical pulling strength:

large amounts of momentum make progression harder to interpret.

Keep the exercise:

* controlled
* repeatable

so your training log actually means something.

---

# Common mistake #2 — using an unnecessarily wide grip

Very wide pull-ups are not automatically:

* harder in a useful way
* better for lats
* better for fighters

Grip-width changes alter joint positions and movement mechanics.

A moderate comfortable grip is enough.

---

# Common mistake #3 — forcing one grip that irritates you

If pronated pull-ups repeatedly bother:

* wrist
* elbow
* shoulder

you do not need to keep them because they are:

**the real pull-up.**

Try:

* neutral grip
* chin-up
* assisted machine
* pulldown

Exercise selection should serve the goal.

---

# Common mistake #4 — chasing maximum reps

Going from:

**8 pull-ups to 10**

is useful progress.

Going from:

**20 to 30**

may matter if pull-up endurance is a personal goal.

But it is not automatically valuable for Muay Thai.

At some point:

adding resistance may provide a clearer strength stimulus.

---

# Common mistake #5 — testing your maximum every session

A maximum pull-up test tells you:

**how many you can do today.**

It is not automatically the best way to train.

Spend most sessions doing:

* repeatable submaximal sets

and test occasionally if you care about the number.

---

# Common mistake #6 — neglecting rows

Pull-ups cover:

**vertical pulling.**

They do not cover every upper-body pulling angle.

A simple program can also include:

* cable row
* dumbbell row
* chest-supported row

You do not need five rowing variations.

One is enough.

---

# Common mistake #7 — adding too much upper-body work

Imagine a week containing:

* pull-ups
* rows
* bench press
* overhead press
* push-ups
* dips
* curls
* triceps
* Muay Thai

That can quickly become more upper-body volume than you need.

Strength work should support:

* skill training

rather than competing with it.

---

# Common mistake #8 — destroying grip before clinch

Pull-ups:

* weighted pull-ups
* rows
* carries

can all fatigue:

* hands
* forearms

If you then have hard clinch training:

your grip may already be tired.

That is not automatically dangerous.

But it may reduce:

**clinch training quality.**

So schedule accordingly.

---

# Common mistake #9 — believing pull-ups prevent shoulder injuries

Pull-ups can strengthen several muscles around the:

* shoulder
* upper back

But no exercise guarantees:

**shoulder injury prevention.**

Injury risk is multifactorial.

Do not sell pull-ups as:

* prehab magic
* shoulder insurance.

Use them as strength training.

---

# Common mistake #10 — expecting direct fight transfer

A stronger pull-up means:

**you became better at pull-up strength.**

That may support broader physical capacity.

It does not prove you will:

* clinch better
* punch harder
* defend better

without specific Muay Thai practice.

---

# Pull-ups and the clinch: realistic expectations

It is reasonable to think that improved:

* pulling strength
* elbow-flexor strength
* grip capacity

may help support an athlete in physical clinch exchanges.

But skill remains dominant.

A technically experienced fighter can:

* manipulate position
* use leverage
* control balance

without necessarily having impressive pull-up numbers.

Likewise:

someone who can do:

**20 pull-ups**

can still be completely lost in the clinch.

Train both:

* physical qualities
* actual skill.

---

# Pull-ups and punching power

Do pull-ups make your punches harder?

There is no good evidence showing a direct relationship.

Punching is a coordinated whole-body action involving:

* ground interaction
* legs
* trunk
* shoulder
* arm
* technique

Pull-ups may contribute to general upper-body strength.

But they are not:

**a punching-power exercise.**

If you want better punches:

practise punching.

Use strength training as support.

---

# Pull-ups and shoulder control

Pull-ups require coordinated movement around the:

* shoulder
* shoulder blade
* elbow

so they can train strength through that movement.

But avoid vague claims that they automatically create:

**better shoulder stability.**

That term can mean many things.

The simplest accurate statement is:

**pull-ups progressively strengthen the muscles involved in vertical pulling.**

That is already useful.

---

# Pull-ups and core strength

Pull-ups also require the trunk to control:

* body position
* swinging

particularly during strict repetitions.

But they are not a complete:

**core exercise.**

If trunk development is a goal:

use dedicated work as well.

For example:

* ab wheel
* Pallof press
* side plank
* carries

Read [Core Training for Fighters: What Actually Builds Power, Stability, and Transfer](/training/core-training-for-fighters/).

Use this if you cannot yet perform:

**3 clean bodyweight pull-ups.**

**3 × 5–8**

### Cable or Machine Row

**2–3 × 8–12**

### Controlled Dead Hang

**1–2 × 15–30 seconds**

if hanging feels comfortable.

When you reach:

**3 × 8**

with good control:

reduce assistance slightly.

Repeat.

# Intermediate pull-up plan

Use this when you can perform several clean bodyweight pull-ups.

Twice per week:

### Pull-Up

**3 × 5–8**

### Row

**3 × 8–12**

That is already enough.

You do not need:

* three extra rear-delt exercises
* five grip drills

unless there is a specific reason.

---

# Stronger athlete plan

If you already have good pull-up strength:

### Weighted Pull-Up

**3–4 × 3–6**

### Row

**2–3 × 6–10**

Optional:

### Bodyweight Pull-Up Back-Off Set

**1 × 6–10**

only if recovery is good.

That provides plenty of vertical and horizontal pulling work.

**one primary pull + one row**

is enough.

Muay Thai still has to fit into the week.

# Should you perform pull-ups before or after other gym exercises?

It depends on priority.

## If pull-up strength is important

Put them earlier.

For example:

after your first main lower-body exercise.

## If pull-ups are accessory work

Put them later.

There is no rule saying:

**fighters must always pull first.**

Use exercise order to reflect your goal.

---

# Should you do pull-ups before Muay Thai?

You can.

But hard sets may fatigue:

* lats
* biceps
* grip
* forearms
* upper back

which may affect:

* clinch
* pad holding
* guard
* punching comfort

later.

If Muay Thai is the important session:

keep pre-training pull-ups:

* light
* low volume

or move them elsewhere.

---

# Are pull-ups a good warm-up?

Hard pull-up sets are:

**training.**

They are not automatically a warm-up.

If you enjoy a few easy repetitions before training:

for example:

**1–2 sets of 3 easy reps**

that can be fine.

But do not perform:

**3 sets to near failure**

and call it activation.

You already trained.

---

# Should pull-ups be done after Muay Thai?

They can be.

But consider what the Muay Thai session contained.

After:

* technical drilling

a short strength block may be manageable.

After:

* hard clinch
* heavy pads
* sparring

pull-up quality may be poor.

The broader principle is:

**put important strength work where you can perform it properly.**

Pull-ups can be part of a gym session performed on the same day as Muay Thai.

That may actually help organise the week by clustering training stress.

For example:

### Tuesday morning

Strength

### Tuesday evening

Muay Thai

### Wednesday

Rest or easy recovery

If you do this:

protect the session that matters most.

# Avoid heavy pull-ups immediately before clinch if quality drops

There is no universal rule saying:

**you must separate pull-ups and clinch by 24 hours.**

But if:

* heavy weighted pull-ups Monday
* clinch Monday evening

consistently gives you:

* weak grip
* tired arms
* poor upper-back endurance

move the strength work.

Your actual response is more useful than an arbitrary rule.

# What if pull-ups make your elbows sore?

Do not keep forcing the same variation.

Try:

* neutral grip
* assisted machine
* pulldown
* lower volume
* lower load

and review the rest of your upper-body work.

If pain is:

* persistent
* worsening
* significant

get appropriate assessment.

This article is not a diagnosis.

---

# What if pull-ups irritate your shoulders?

Again:

change the exercise if needed.

Options include:

* neutral-grip pulldown
* assisted neutral-grip pull-up
* comfortable machine pull

You do not need to earn fighter credibility by using one particular exercise.

---

# What if you cannot feel your lats?

You do not need to:

**feel a dramatic lat burn**

for pull-ups to train them.

EMG studies clearly show lat activity during pull-up variations.

Focus on:

* controlled repetitions
* appropriate range
* progression

rather than chasing a sensation.

---

# What if your grip fails before your back?

That can happen.

Options include:

* continue training grip through the exercise
* use fewer reps
* strengthen grip separately if it is a genuine limitation

Straps can also reduce grip limitation in some pulling exercises.

But for pull-ups:

most fighters can simply let grip be part of the exercise unless it prevents the intended training completely.

---

# Should fighters use lifting straps for pull-ups?

Usually unnecessary.

But there is no rule against them.

If you specifically want to train:

**vertical pulling muscles**

and grip is currently the only limiting factor:

straps may change the limiting factor.

On the other hand:

if general grip strength is useful to your goals:

training without straps may be preferable.

This is programming.

Not morality.

---

# Do pull-ups improve grip endurance?

They challenge grip.

But if your goal is specifically:

**long-duration grip endurance,**

pull-ups are not the only tool.

Other options include:

* carries
* hangs
* rows
* sport-specific clinch work

Use the method that matches the goal.

---

# Are dead hangs useful?

They can be used for:

* grip exposure
* tolerance to hanging
* simple shoulder movement

But they are not mandatory.

Do not assume dead hangs:

* decompress the spine permanently
* fix shoulders
* prevent injuries

Use them if:

* comfortable
* useful for your goal.

---

# What about scapular pull-ups?

They can be useful as:

* a movement drill
* lower-load pulling preparation

But you do not need a long:

**scapular activation routine**

before every pull-up session.

A few controlled reps may be enough if they help you prepare.

---

# What about towel pull-ups?

Towel pull-ups increase the grip challenge.

That can be interesting for:

* grip strength

but they are more demanding on:

* hands
* forearms

and are not required for Muay Thai.

A towel does not make the exercise:

**clinch-specific.**

If you enjoy them:

use them occasionally.

Do not mistake superficial similarity for direct sport transfer.

---

# What about rope pull-ups?

Same principle.

Rope gripping changes:

* hand position
* grip demand

Research comparing rope and other pull-up variations shows differences in some muscle activation measures, but not a completely different exercise.

Use rope pull-ups if:

* you have the equipment
* you want the grip challenge

not because fighters:

**need rope work.**

# Pull-ups versus lat pulldowns

A pulldown is not a:

**beginner-only substitute.**

It is a valid resistance exercise.

## Pull-ups

Resistance is largely based on:

* your body mass
* any added load

## Pulldowns

Load is externally adjustable.

That can make progression easier.

Both train vertical pulling.

## When pulldowns may be useful

If:

* bodyweight is currently too heavy for good pull-ups
* you want smaller load increments
* elbows or shoulders tolerate pulldowns better
* fatigue is high

use them.

There is no need to prove anything by forcing pull-ups.

# Pull-ups versus chin-ups

Neither is universally better.

A 2010 EMG study found greater:

* biceps
* pectoralis major

activity during chin-ups compared with pull-ups, while lower trapezius activity was higher in pull-ups.

Another pull-up-variation study found many similarities across grip orientations.

So choose according to:

* comfort
* progression
* training goal

rather than internet mythology.

**fighter strength.**

You do not need to maximise lat size for Muay Thai.

Some muscle development is a normal adaptation to resistance training.

# FAQ

## Are pull-ups good for Muay Thai?

Yes.

They are a useful vertical-pulling strength exercise.

## Do pull-ups help clinch?

They may build general pulling and grip qualities that can support clinch work.

But there is no direct evidence that improving pull-up performance automatically improves Muay Thai clinch performance.

Clinch skill must be trained through clinching.

## Do pull-ups improve punching power?

There is no good evidence showing a direct effect.

Punching is a coordinated whole-body skill.

## What muscles do pull-ups work?

Major contributors include:

* lats
* biceps
* brachioradialis
* trapezius
* shoulder musculature
* forearms

with the trunk also helping control body position.

## Are pull-ups good for grip?

They challenge grip.

Yes.

But bar grip is not the same as clinch grip.

## How many pull-ups should a fighter do?

A practical starting point is:

**2–4 sets**

once or twice per week.

The exact amount depends on:

* current strength
* Muay Thai volume
* other gym work.

## What rep range should fighters use?

For bodyweight pull-ups:

**4–8 reps**

is a useful strength-focused range.

Higher reps can also be used.

Weighted pull-ups often fit well around:

**3–6 reps.**

These are practical ranges, not biological rules.

## How many RIR?

Most working sets can finish around:

**1–3 RIR.**

## Should fighters train pull-ups to failure?

Not routinely.

Failure is not required for strength development and creates greater acute fatigue.

## Are weighted pull-ups good for fighters?

They can be.

Use them when:

* bodyweight pull-ups are easy
* recovery is good
* you want more pulling strength.

## How much weight should I add?

Start small.

Often:

**2.5–5 kg**

is enough for the first progression.

## Do fighters need 20 pull-ups?

No.

That can be a personal fitness goal.

It is not a Muay Thai requirement.

## What if I cannot do one pull-up?

Use:

* assisted machine
* band assistance
* pulldowns

and progressively reduce assistance.

## Are negative pull-ups useful?

Yes, particularly as one progression tool toward a full pull-up.

Keep eccentric volume modest.

## Are chin-ups better?

No universal advantage.

They are simply another vertical pulling variation.

## Is neutral grip better?

Not universally.

Use it if:

* comfortable
* easy to progress.

## Are wide-grip pull-ups better for lats?

There is no need for an extreme wide grip.

A comfortable moderate grip works well.

## Should I do pull-ups and rows?

You can.

They train different pulling directions.

## Should I do pull-ups every day?

Usually unnecessary.

## Should I do pull-ups before Muay Thai?

Hard sets can fatigue the upper body.

If Muay Thai is the priority:

keep them easy or schedule them elsewhere.

## Can I do pull-ups after Muay Thai?

Yes, if:

* you still have enough quality
* the session was not already extremely demanding.

## Should I do pull-ups before clinch?

Avoid hard pull-up work immediately beforehand if it consistently reduces:

* grip
* pulling capacity
* clinch quality.

## Do pull-ups protect shoulders?

Do not treat them as guaranteed injury prevention.

They are simply a resistance-training exercise for vertical pulling.

## Do pull-ups fix posture?

No.

They strengthen muscles involved in pulling.

Posture is more complicated than one exercise.

## Do pull-ups train core?

The trunk helps control body position.

But pull-ups are not a complete trunk-training program.

## Are towel pull-ups good for Muay Thai?

They create more grip demand.

They are optional.

They are not proven to improve clinch performance more than standard pulling work.

## Are rope pull-ups useful?

They can be.

Again:

optional.

## Are lat pulldowns a good substitute?

Yes.

Especially if you need:

* scalable resistance
* lower relative difficulty
* more controlled progression.

## Should fighters use straps?

Usually unnecessary for pull-ups.

But they can be used if grip is limiting the specific pulling stimulus you want.

## How long does it take to get your first pull-up?

There is no reliable universal timeline.

It depends on:

* body mass
* current strength
* training frequency
* previous experience.

Track progress rather than chasing a deadline.

## Final takeaway

Pull-ups can absolutely have a place in a Muay Thai strength program.

They give you a simple way to progressively train:

* vertical pulling strength
* lats
* upper back
* biceps
* forearms
* grip

Those qualities may support the broader physical demands of:

* clinch
* hand fighting
* upper-body control

but pull-ups are not:

**clinch practice.**

They also do not automatically improve:

* punching power
* striking technique
* fighting ability.

Start with:

* assisted pull-ups if needed

progress to:

* clean bodyweight reps

and add:

* modest external load

when bodyweight work becomes easy.

Keep most sets:

**short of failure.**

Use enough pulling volume to become stronger.

Then leave enough recovery to actually:

**train Muay Thai well.**
