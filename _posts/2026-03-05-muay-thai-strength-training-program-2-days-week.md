If you train Muay Thai several times per week, you probably do not need:

**another four or five hard gym days.**

You need enough strength training to improve useful physical qualities without turning every Muay Thai session into a battle against gym fatigue.

That is where a simple:

**2-day full body strength program**

works well.

Two gym sessions can give you regular exposure to:

* lower-body strength
* hip and posterior-chain strength
* upper-body pushing
* upper-body pulling
* single-leg work
* trunk strength
* power work

while leaving most of the week available for:

* technique
* pads
* bag work
* clinch
* sparring
* recovery

This article gives you the actual program.

Not just a list of good exercises.

You will get:

* Day A
* Day B
* sets and reps
* repetitions in reserve
* rest periods
* exercise substitutions
* a simple progression system
* weekly schedules for 2, 3, or 4 Muay Thai sessions
* adjustments for hard sparring weeks
* a reduced-fatigue version when recovery is poor

If you want the bigger picture of how to organise the entire week first, read [Muay Thai Workout Plan: How to Build a Weekly Gym Program Without Ruining Your Fight Training](/training/muay-thai-workout-plan-weekly-gym-program/).

## Who this program is for

This plan is designed for someone who:

* trains Muay Thai regularly
* can lift twice per week
* wants useful strength rather than bodybuilding-only training
* wants a simple repeatable program
* needs to manage soreness and fatigue
* wants gym work to support rather than replace Muay Thai

It is particularly practical if you train Muay Thai:

**2–4 times per week.**

It can also be adapted for:

* boxing
* kickboxing
* other striking sports

but the examples here are written around Muay Thai.

## Who this program is not for

This is not:

* a professional fight-camp peak
* a powerlifting program
* a bodybuilding split
* a rehabilitation plan
* a maximum-strength specialization block

If you have:

* an acute injury
* significant pain
* post-operative restrictions
* medical limitations

use an appropriately individualized program.

## Why two full-body days?

There is nothing magical about:

**two.**

It is simply a useful compromise.

Resistance training can improve:

* strength
* power
* muscle function

and combat-sport research supports including strength training as part of athletic preparation.

But Muay Thai already creates substantial training stress.

So the strength program needs to fit around:

* kicks
* knees
* clinch
* sparring
* conditioning
* technical practice

rather than pretending the gym exists in isolation.

For many recreational fighters, two full-body sessions provide enough exposure to progress while keeping the total weekly workload manageable.

## Two gym days are a starting structure, not a universal optimum

Some people may do well with:

**one gym session**

during very hard Muay Thai periods.

Others may tolerate:

**three.**

Your ideal frequency depends on:

* Muay Thai frequency
* session intensity
* gym experience
* sleep
* nutrition
* life stress
* competition schedule
* recovery capacity

The reason two days works so well practically is that you can train most major movement patterns twice across the week without needing a separate:

* leg day
* push day
* pull day
* shoulder day

That keeps the plan compact.

## The main rule

If Muay Thai is your priority:

**gym performance should not repeatedly improve while Muay Thai performance deteriorates.**

The goal is not to become the best gym athlete in your Muay Thai class.

The goal is to become:

* stronger
* more powerful
* physically better prepared

while still being able to:

* kick well
* move well
* spar well
* learn skills

## What this program trains

Across the two sessions you will cover:

### Lower-body strength

Squat and hinge patterns.

### Single-leg strength

Useful because Muay Thai constantly places you in asymmetrical positions.

### Horizontal push and pull

General upper-body strength.

### Vertical or angled push and pull

Additional shoulder and upper-back strength.

### Trunk control

Bracing, anti-rotation and load transfer.

### Power

Low-volume explosive work performed while fresh.

### Optional support work

Only if recovery allows it.

## The weekly program

The base version is:

### Day A

**Strength emphasis**

### Day B

**Power + strength emphasis**

Both sessions are full body.

You do not need a separate leg day.

## Day A — Strength emphasis

| Exercise | Sets | Reps | Effort |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Main lower-body lift | 3 | 4–6 | 2–3 RIR |
| Horizontal push | 3 | 5–8 | 1–3 RIR |
| Horizontal pull | 3 | 6–10 | 1–3 RIR |
| Single-leg movement | 2 | 6–8/side | 2–3 RIR |
| Trunk exercise | 2–3 | 6–12 or timed | Controlled |
| Optional carry | 2 | 20–40 m | Moderate |

RIR means:

**repetitions in reserve.**

So:

**2 RIR**

means you finish the set believing you could have completed approximately two more good repetitions.

RIR is not perfectly precise.

It is simply a useful way to control effort.

## 1. Main lower-body lift

Choose one:

* Front Squat
* Back Squat
* Trap-Bar Deadlift
* Goblet Squat
* Leg Press

Perform:

**3 sets × 4–6 reps**

at approximately:

**2–3 RIR**

Rest:

**2–4 minutes**

between hard working sets.

## Why only one main lower-body lift?

Because you still have Muay Thai.

You do not need:

* heavy squats
* heavy deadlifts
* hack squats
* leg press

all in the same session to prove that you trained legs.

One properly loaded main movement can provide a strong training stimulus.

You will add another lower-body pattern later in the week.

## Front squat

A front squat can work well if you:

* tolerate it comfortably
* have the necessary technique
* want a squat-dominant movement

It is not inherently more:

**functional**

for Muay Thai than every other squat.

It is simply a good option.

## Trap-bar deadlift

The trap bar can be useful if:

* your gym has one
* you are comfortable with the movement
* you want a combined leg and hip-dominant exercise

Again:

there is no requirement for Muay Thai athletes to trap-bar deadlift.

Use it if it suits you.

## Goblet squat

A goblet squat is particularly useful for:

* newer lifters
* lighter training weeks
* limited equipment
* learning squat mechanics

Its biggest limitation is that the dumbbell may eventually become harder to hold than the movement is for your legs.

At that point:

use another squat variation.

## 2. Horizontal push

Choose one:

* Dumbbell Bench Press
* Barbell Bench Press
* Incline Dumbbell Press
* Push-Up with added resistance if needed

Perform:

**3 sets × 5–8 reps**

at:

**1–3 RIR**

Rest:

**2–3 minutes**

if the sets are demanding.

## Do fighters need bench press?

No.

Bench press is simply one way to develop:

* chest
* shoulders
* triceps
* general pressing strength

You can use dumbbells or push-ups instead.

Strength training does not need to imitate punching mechanically to be useful.

## Gym exercises do not need to look exactly like Muay Thai

This is an important principle.

The purpose of the gym is not to recreate:

* punches with cables
* kicks with ankle weights
* clinch movements under random resistance

Muay Thai already provides specific practice.

The gym builds general physical qualities.

Let each form of training do its own job.

## 3. Horizontal pull

Choose one:

* Chest-Supported Row
* Seated Cable Row
* One-Arm Dumbbell Row
* Machine Row

Perform:

**3 sets × 6–10 reps**

at:

**1–3 RIR**

Rest:

**90 seconds to 3 minutes**

depending on difficulty.

## Why pulling belongs here

Pulling strengthens areas including:

* upper back
* elbow flexors
* rear shoulder musculature

That can complement:

* pressing
* punching volume
* clinch demands

But there is no evidence-based rule saying fighters must perform:

**two pulling exercises for every pushing exercise.**

Use sensible overall programming rather than arbitrary ratios.

## 4. Single-leg movement

Choose one:

* Rear-Foot-Elevated Split Squat
* Reverse Lunge
* Step-Up
* Regular Split Squat

Perform:

**2 sets × 6–8 reps per side**

at:

**2–3 RIR**

Rest:

**60–120 seconds**

between sides or sets as needed.

## Why only two sets?

Single-leg exercises can create considerable soreness when:

* new
* performed deeply
* loaded aggressively

especially when combined with Muay Thai.

Two good sets may be enough.

If you tolerate them well and want more lower-body development:

you can eventually add a third set.

Do not begin with maximum volume.

## If split squats destroy your legs

Do not conclude:

**“fighters should not lift legs.”**

Try:

* fewer sets
* lighter load
* less aggressive range initially
* a different exercise
* keeping the exercise consistently rather than changing it every week

For the full leg-training discussion, read [Should You Train Legs If You Do Muay Thai? Strength, Soreness, and Smart Programming](/training/should-you-train-legs-with-muay-thai/).

## 5. Trunk exercise

Choose one:

* Pallof Press
* Dead Bug
* Ab Wheel
* Cable Anti-Rotation Hold

Perform:

**2–3 sets**

using controlled repetitions.

Examples:

### Pallof Press

**8–12 reps per side**

### Dead Bug

**6–10 reps per side**

### Ab Wheel

**5–10 reps**

provided you can control the range.

## Core training is not just sit-ups

Muay Thai involves:

* trunk rotation
* force transfer
* bracing
* resisting unwanted movement

So your strength work can include different trunk functions.

That does not mean:

**anti-rotation is automatically more functional than rotation.**

You can train both across your wider program.

For a dedicated guide, read [Core Training for Fighters: Strength, Rotation, and Trunk Control](/training/core-training-for-fighters/).

## 6. Optional carry

If recovery is good:

choose:

* Farmer Carry
* Suitcase Carry

Perform:

**2 rounds × 20–40 metres**

This is optional.

Carries can train:

* grip
* trunk
* general loaded movement

But you do not need to finish every gym session with a:

**finisher.**

If you have hard Muay Thai later or tomorrow:

skip it.

## Day A should not destroy you

The session should feel like:

**productive strength work.**

Not:

**a leg-day survival challenge.**

You should generally leave with the feeling that:

* you trained hard
* technique remained good
* you could still function normally

You do not need to crawl out of the gym.

## Day B — Power + strength emphasis

| Exercise | Sets | Reps | Effort |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Power movement | 3–4 | Low reps | Fast, fresh |
| Hinge | 3 | 5–8 | 2–3 RIR |
| Vertical pull | 3 | 5–10 | 1–3 RIR |
| Angled/vertical press | 2–3 | 6–10 | 2–3 RIR |
| Hamstring/adductor support | 2 | 8–12 or timed | Controlled |
| Trunk/carry | 2–3 | Variable | Controlled |
| Optional shoulder support | 1–2 | 10–20 | Easy |

## 1. Power movement

Do this:

**first after your warm-up.**

Choose one:

### Box Jump

**3–4 sets × 2–3 reps**

### Countermovement Jump

**3–4 sets × 3 reps**

### Rotational Medicine-Ball Throw

**3–4 sets × 3–5 reps per side**

### Medicine-Ball Slam

**3–4 sets × 4–6 reps**

### Kettlebell Swing

**3 sets × 6–10 reps**

if you already know the technique.

## Power training should not feel like conditioning

The goal is:

**speed and quality.**

Stop the set when:

* jumps clearly lose height
* throws become slow
* movement loses sharpness

Do not perform:

**30 explosive reps because more must be better.**

The moment power work becomes:

* breathless
* grinding
* slow

you are training something else.

## Why power work comes first

Explosive movements depend on producing force quickly.

So you want to perform them:

* before significant fatigue
* with good technique
* with adequate rest

Do not put jumps after:

* heavy squats
* lunges
* conditioning circuits

and then wonder why they look slow.

## Power work is optional

You can still run a very good two-day strength program without:

* box jumps
* medicine balls
* kettlebells

If you are:

* new to lifting
* very fatigued
* unfamiliar with explosive exercises

skip them.

Strength itself can contribute to the physical base for power.

You can add dedicated power work later.

## 2. Hinge movement

Choose one:

* Romanian Deadlift
* Hip Thrust
* Trap-Bar Deadlift if you did not use it on Day A
* Cable Pull-Through for a lighter option

Perform:

**3 sets × 5–8 reps**

at:

**2–3 RIR**

For hip thrusts you can also use:

**6–10 reps.**

## Romanian deadlift

A Romanian deadlift can provide useful loading for:

* hamstrings
* glutes
* hip extensors

But it can create substantial soreness if:

* the exercise is new
* volume is high
* you suddenly increase range

Start conservatively.

## Day A and Day B should complement each other

For example:

### If Day A uses a squat

Day B can use an RDL.

### If Day A uses a trap-bar deadlift

Day B could use:

* a lighter squat
* hip thrust

The sessions should not simply repeat:

**two maximal lower-body days.**

## 3. Vertical pull

Choose one:

* Pull-Up
* Assisted Pull-Up
* Lat Pulldown
* Neutral-Grip Pulldown

Perform:

**3 sets × 5–10 reps**

at:

**1–3 RIR**

If you can easily exceed the range on pull-ups:

add resistance gradually.

If you cannot yet complete clean pull-ups:

use:

* assistance
* pulldown

There is no prize for ugly repetitions.

## 4. Angled or vertical press

Choose one:

* Landmine Press
* Dumbbell Overhead Press
* Half-Kneeling Landmine Press
* Machine Shoulder Press if comfortable

Perform:

**2–3 sets × 6–10 reps**

at:

**2–3 RIR**

## Landmine press is an option, not a corrective exercise

Some fighters find the angled pressing path comfortable.

That is enough reason to use it.

Do not claim it:

* fixes shoulders
* prevents injury
* is automatically superior to overhead pressing

Choose the press you tolerate and can progress.

## If shoulders are already fatigued

After:

* heavy pads
* bag work
* clinch

you may reduce pressing volume.

For example:

**2 sets instead of 3.**

Or choose a comfortable variation.

For mobility-specific work, read [Shoulder Mobility for Fighters: A Practical Routine for Boxing and Muay Thai](/recovery/shoulder-mobility-for-fighters/).

## 5. Hamstring or adductor support

Choose one:

### Leg Curl

**2 sets × 8–12 reps**

### Short-Lever Copenhagen Plank

**2 sets × 15–25 seconds per side**

### Adductor Machine

**2 sets × 8–15 reps**

## Start adductor training conservatively

Kicking and clinching already expose the:

* groin
* adductors

to considerable work.

If Copenhagen planks are new:

do not begin with:

* maximal lever length
* huge volume

and then complain that walking hurts.

Start easy.

## 6. Trunk or carry

Choose one:

* Side Plank
* Suitcase Carry
* Pallof Hold
* Farmer Carry

Examples:

### Side Plank

**2 sets × 20–40 seconds per side**

### Suitcase Carry

**2–3 rounds × 20–30 metres per side**

Keep this controlled.

Do not turn carries into:

**conditioning races.**

## 7. Optional shoulder support

Choose one if you want it:

* Band External Rotation
* Face Pull
* Rear-Delt Raise

Perform:

**1–2 sets × 10–20 reps**

at easy-to-moderate effort.

This is support work.

Not another main lift.

## The full program at a glance

### Day A

1. Main lower-body lift — **3 × 4–6**
2. Horizontal push — **3 × 5–8**
3. Horizontal pull — **3 × 6–10**
4. Single-leg movement — **2 × 6–8/side**
5. Trunk — **2–3 sets**
6. Optional carry — **2 rounds**

### Day B

1. Power movement — **3–4 low-rep sets**
2. Hinge — **3 × 5–8**
3. Vertical pull — **3 × 5–10**
4. Angled/vertical press — **2–3 × 6–10**
5. Hamstring/adductor support — **2 sets**
6. Trunk/carry — **2–3 sets**
7. Optional shoulder support — **1–2 sets**

That is enough.

## How long should each session take?

For most people:

**45–70 minutes**

is realistic.

You may finish faster if:

* warm-up is short
* equipment is available
* optional work is skipped

You may take longer if:

* the gym is busy
* you need more rest
* you are using heavier compound lifts

Do not shorten rest between major strength sets just to make the session:

**more athletic.**

If quality matters:

rest enough.

## Warm-up

You do not need an elaborate 30-minute routine.

A practical warm-up:

### 3–5 minutes

Easy:

* bike
* walk
* rower

if you like it.

### Then

Choose a few relevant movements:

* ankle movement
* hip movement
* thoracic rotation
* shoulder movement

### Then

Do:

**specific warm-up sets**

for the first main lift.

Those ramp-up sets are part of your warm-up.

## Example squat warm-up

If your working sets are:

**80 kg**

you might perform something like:

* empty bar
* 40 kg
* 60 kg
* 70 kg

before your first working set.

The exact numbers depend on:

* your strength
* exercise
* experience

The aim is to arrive at the working load prepared.

Not tired.

## You do not need to stretch everything before lifting

If a specific mobility drill helps you:

use it.

But do not create a universal ritual involving:

* every joint
* every muscle
* 15 stretches

unless you genuinely need it.

For a short general routine, use [10-Minute Mobility Routine: Daily Reset for Hips, Ankles, and Upper Back](/recovery/10-minute-mobility-routine/).

## How hard should the gym sessions be?

Most working sets should be:

**challenging but controlled.**

A good starting target is:

**1–3 repetitions in reserve.**

For large compound lifts:

**2–3 RIR**

is often particularly useful when Muay Thai volume is high.

This gives you room to:

* maintain technique
* avoid unnecessary grinders
* control fatigue

## Why not train everything to failure?

Because failure is not required for strength development.

Systematic reviews comparing failure and non-failure resistance training have not shown that reaching failure is necessary for gaining strength.

Training to failure also creates more acute fatigue.

That matters when strength training is not your only sport.

You still need to:

* kick
* move
* spar

afterwards.

## Does that mean failure is forbidden?

No.

An occasional final set of:

* curls
* small accessory work

taken close to failure is not going to destroy your program.

The point is:

**do not build the whole two-day plan around maximal effort on every set.**

There is a difference between:

* training hard

and:

* maximizing fatigue.

## RIR does not need to be perfectly accurate

You may think:

**“How do I know whether I had exactly two reps left?”**

You don't.

It is an estimate.

With experience:

you become better at judging it.

The purpose is simply to distinguish:

**comfortable**

from:

**challenging**

from:

**absolute failure.**

For this program:

you mostly want:

**challenging, repeatable sets.**

## Progression: use rep ranges

The easiest method for this plan is:

**double progression.**

Imagine your exercise is:

**3 × 5–8.**

Start with a load you can perform for:

**3 × 5**

with the target RIR.

Across later sessions, gradually build reps.

For example:

### Week 1

7 / 6 / 5

### Week 2

7 / 7 / 6

### Week 3

8 / 7 / 7

### Week 4

8 / 8 / 8

Now increase the load modestly.

Then you may return to:

**5–6 reps**

and build again.

## Do not force progression every session

Muay Thai changes week to week.

One week you may have:

* technical classes

The next:

* brutal sparring
* hard clinch
* extra conditioning

Your gym progression will not always be perfectly linear.

That is normal.

## Three ways to progress

You can progress by increasing:

### Repetitions

Example:

6 → 7 → 8.

### Load

Example:

60 kg → 62.5 kg.

### Quality

Same load and reps but:

* better control
* better range
* more stable technique
* lower perceived effort

All of these can be useful signs of progress.

For the full explanation, read [Progressive Overload Explained: How to Keep Getting Stronger Without Guessing](/training/progressive-overload-explained-how-to-keep-getting-stronger/).

## Do not increase everything simultaneously

If you add:

* weight
* reps
* sets

at the same time:

fatigue can rise very quickly.

Progress gradually.

The gym program should remain boring enough to measure.

## How to schedule the two sessions

There is no one correct week.

Protect your hardest or most important Muay Thai sessions.

## Option A — Muay Thai twice per week

### Monday

Gym A

### Tuesday

Muay Thai

### Wednesday

Rest or easy recovery

### Thursday

Gym B

### Friday

Muay Thai

### Saturday

Easy activity or rest

### Sunday

Rest

This gives plenty of recovery space.

## Option B — Muay Thai three times per week

### Monday

Muay Thai

### Tuesday

Gym A

### Wednesday

Muay Thai

### Thursday

Rest or active recovery

### Friday

Gym B

### Saturday

Muay Thai

### Sunday

Rest

This can work well if Saturday is not consistently ruined by Friday's gym session.

If it is:

change Friday.

For example:

* reduce lower-body volume
* move Gym B
* put the harder gym session earlier in the week

The template is not sacred.

## Option C — Muay Thai four times per week

### Monday

Muay Thai

### Tuesday

Gym A

### Wednesday

Muay Thai

### Thursday

Gym B

### Friday

Muay Thai

### Saturday

Muay Thai

### Sunday

Rest

This is a much denser week.

So gym volume may need to be:

**lower.**

If Saturday is your main sparring session:

do not blindly keep Thursday's full lower-body workload just because the spreadsheet says so.

## A better schedule if Saturday sparring matters most

### Monday

Muay Thai

### Tuesday

Gym A

### Wednesday

Muay Thai

### Thursday

Gym B — reduced lower-body volume

### Friday

Rest or technical/easy work

### Saturday

Sparring

### Sunday

Rest

Now Friday protects Saturday.

The exact answer depends on your class schedule.

## Avoid hard lower-body work directly before key sparring

There is no universal rule saying:

**you need exactly 48 hours.**

Recovery varies.

But if experience shows that:

* split squats Friday
* sparring Saturday

makes you:

* slow
* sore
* mechanically poor

then the schedule is telling you something.

Move or reduce the leg work.

## Same-day gym + Muay Thai

Sometimes two sessions on one day actually make weekly scheduling easier.

Example:

### Tuesday

Gym

then several hours later:

Muay Thai

### Wednesday

Rest

instead of spreading stress across:

* Tuesday
* Wednesday

This can work.

But manage the dose.

## Which session should come first?

Prioritize what matters most.

If Muay Thai skill is the main goal:

doing Muay Thai fresher often makes sense.

If strength is currently the main developmental priority:

gym may come first.

There is no universal order.

If possible:

separate the sessions by several hours.

For practical recovery between sessions, read [Two Workouts a Day: How to Recover Between Training Sessions](/recovery/recover-between-two-training-sessions-in-one-day/).

## The hard-sparring-week version

If sparring volume suddenly increases:

reduce gym stress.

Do not automatically remove strength training completely.

A lower-volume version may maintain the habit and much of the stimulus.

## Day A — Reduced version

1. Main lower-body lift — **2 × 4–6**
2. Horizontal push — **2 × 5–8**
3. Horizontal pull — **2 × 6–10**
4. Trunk — **2 sets**

Done.

## Day B — Reduced version

1. Optional power movement — **2–3 short sets**
2. Hinge — **2 × 5–8**
3. Vertical pull — **2 × 6–10**
4. Press — **2 × 6–10**
5. Trunk — **2 sets**

No:

* finishers
* extra leg work
* random conditioning

You still train.

You simply reduce fatigue.

## The very-fatigued week

If you have several signs together:

* poor sleep
* heavy legs
* falling gym performance
* poor Muay Thai performance
* unusual soreness
* low motivation

you do not need to force both full sessions.

Possible choices:

* one reduced session
* two very short maintenance sessions
* a deload week

depending on the pattern.

For the recovery decision, read [How Many Rest Days Do You Really Need? Gym + Muay Thai Recovery](/recovery/how-many-rest-days-per-week-gym-muay-thai/).

For accumulated fatigue, read [Deload Week for Muay Thai + Gym: When to Do It and How to Do It](/recovery/deload-week-muay-thai-gym/).

## What to remove first when fatigue rises

Do not immediately delete:

**all strength training.**

Remove the lowest-priority fatigue first.

A practical order is:

### 1. Finishers

Easy decision.

### 2. Optional accessory work

Shoulder extras, carries, extra isolation work.

### 3. Third sets on secondary exercises

Move:

**3 → 2 sets.**

### 4. Single-leg volume

Especially if it creates large DOMS.

### 5. Power work

If movement is no longer fast.

### 6. Main-lift volume

Keep a few good sets rather than a full normal session.

This gives you a scalable program.

## Beginner version

If you are new to resistance training:

do not start with the full program at maximum volume.

Use:

### Day A

* Lower-body lift — **2 × 6–8**
* Push — **2 × 6–10**
* Pull — **2 × 8–10**
* Simple trunk — **2 sets**

### Day B

* Hinge — **2 × 6–8**
* Pull — **2 × 8–10**
* Press — **2 × 8–10**
* Split squat or step-up — **1–2 × 8/side**
* Trunk — **2 sets**

Skip power training until:

* basic exercise technique
* regular gym attendance

are established.

## Beginners do not need more complexity

The first goal is:

**learn the movements and repeat the program.**

Not:

* advanced periodization
* velocity tracking
* chains
* bands
* contrast training
* five power exercises

If you want a general beginner-focused program, read [Beginner Strength Program: 3 Days Per Week With Simple Progression](/training/beginner-strength-program-3-days-week/).

### Hinge slot

Replace with:

* Romanian deadlift
* trap-bar deadlift
* hip thrust
* cable pull-through

### Horizontal push

Replace with:

* dumbbell press
* bench press
* incline dumbbell press
* weighted push-up

### Horizontal pull

Replace with:

* cable row
* chest-supported row
* dumbbell row
* machine row

### Vertical pull

Replace with:

* pull-up
* assisted pull-up
* pulldown

### Single-leg

Replace with:

* split squat
* step-up
* reverse lunge

## You do not need "the best exercise"

If an exercise:

* hurts
* does not fit your anatomy
* is unavailable
* creates disproportionate fatigue

choose another.

Resistance training works through:

* progressive loading
* sufficient effort
* repeated exposure

not loyalty to one particular barbell exercise.

## What about machines?

Machines are completely acceptable.

Current resistance-training evidence does not support the idea that only:

**free weights**

produce useful adaptations.

If a machine lets you train:

* hard
* safely
* consistently

it can be part of the program.

## What about Olympic lifts?

You do not need:

* cleans
* snatches

to become a strong fighter.

They can develop power when:

* coached properly
* performed well

but they also require skill.

If you already know them:

they can be used.

If you do not:

you can build power with simpler options such as:

* jumps
* throws

without spending months learning Olympic lifting.

## What about kettlebells?

Kettlebells are useful tools.

They are not mandatory.

A kettlebell swing can train:

* rapid hip extension

but if your swing technique is poor:

use something else.

The tool is less important than the training goal.

## Should you add conditioning to this program?

Usually not by default.

Muay Thai itself already provides substantial conditioning.

If you need additional aerobic work:

program it separately and deliberately.

Do not automatically add:

**10-minute death circuits**

after each strength session.

The gym plan is primarily:

**strength and power support.**

## Zone 2 after lifting?

A short easy session may fit if:

* recovery is good
* aerobic development is a goal

But it is extra training load.

Do not assume:

**easy cardio has zero cost.**

If Muay Thai volume is already high:

you may not need it.

## How much rest between sets?

For major strength exercises:

usually:

**2–4 minutes**

is reasonable when needed.

For smaller accessory exercises:

roughly:

**60–120 seconds**

may be enough.

For power exercises:

rest until you can produce another:

**fast, high-quality set.**

Do not force short rest just because:

**fighters need conditioning.**

Muay Thai trains conditioning.

Strength sessions should allow strength-quality work.

## What about supersets?

You can use them to save time.

Good combinations include exercises that interfere minimally.

For example:

### Pair

Bench Press  
+  
Cable Row

or:

Landmine Press  
+  
Trunk exercise

Avoid supersets that turn the session into a conditioning circuit and reduce performance on the main lifts.

## How to know whether the program is working

Track a few simple things.

### Gym

Are:

* loads
* reps
* technique

gradually improving?

### Muay Thai

Are you still able to:

* kick normally
* move normally
* spar with quality

### Recovery

Are you generally recovering between sessions?

### Soreness

Is it manageable?

You do not need:

* perfect readiness scores
* laboratory testing

to see whether the plan fits.

## When the program is too much

Watch for a repeated pattern of:

* leg soreness ruining Muay Thai
* declining lifting performance
* declining sparring performance
* persistent heavy legs
* unusually poor sleep
* accumulating fatigue
* constantly needing to skip sessions

One poor workout means very little.

A repeated pattern means:

**adjust the program.**

## When the program may be too little

If after several weeks:

* every session feels very easy
* loads are not progressing
* sets finish with huge numbers of reps in reserve
* you recover easily

you may need:

* more load
* more reps
* occasionally another set

Do not add five new exercises.

Progress the existing program first.

## Do you need a deload every four weeks?

No.

You can use planned deloads.

But there is no universal biological rule requiring one at an exact interval.

Reduce training when:

* fatigue accumulates
* performance drops
* your fight schedule changes
* the next block demands more Muay Thai work

The plan should fit reality.

## What about fight camp?

Closer to competition, priorities may shift.

You may have:

* more sparring
* more pads
* tactical work
* weight-management demands

Strength training may become:

* shorter
* lower volume
* more maintenance-oriented

This article is not a fight-camp peaking protocol.

Do not insist on progressing your gym lifts aggressively while the sport-specific workload is peaking.

## Recovery between gym sessions

You do not need elaborate recovery tools.

The main factors remain:

* enough sleep
* adequate food
* sensible training load
* hydration
* time

Read [Sleep After Training: How to Recover Better Without Fancy Gadgets](/recovery/sleep-after-training-how-to-recover-faster-and-perform-better/) if sleep is the weak point.

## Soreness is not the goal

You should not judge Day A by whether:

**stairs hurt for three days.**

Soreness is influenced by:

* novelty
* eccentric loading
* volume
* exercise selection

You can make strength progress without severe DOMS.

## DOMS vs pain

Mild general muscle soreness after lifting may be normal.

Be more cautious with:

* sudden sharp pain
* localized pain
* significant weakness
* swelling
* bruising
* pain that changes normal movement

If you are unsure, read [DOMS vs Strain: The Simple Difference Between Muscle Soreness and Injury](/recovery/doms-vs-strain/).

## Common mistake #1: adding too much volume

You find a 2-day program.

Then add:

* curls
* triceps
* extra chest
* extra legs
* calves
* another hamstring exercise
* conditioning
* abs
* five shoulder drills

Now it is no longer the same program.

Start with the base.

Add only what solves a real need.

## Common mistake #2: taking every set to failure

You do not need this for strength.

And it increases fatigue.

Keep most compound work:

**short of failure.**

## Common mistake #3: turning power work into cardio

Ten fast jumps can make sense.

Fifty fatigued jumps are a different session.

Protect movement quality.

## Common mistake #4: changing exercises every week

Progress becomes difficult to measure when:

* front squat becomes back squat
* then hack squat
* then split squat
* then safety bar

every seven days.

Keep main movements stable long enough to:

* learn them
* progress them
* evaluate them

## Common mistake #5: copying a bodybuilder

A bodybuilding program may deliberately maximize:

* hypertrophy volume
* local muscular fatigue

That is appropriate for bodybuilding.

A Muay Thai-focused program has another constraint:

**you still need quality sport practice.**

## Common mistake #6: copying a powerlifter

If Muay Thai is your priority:

you do not need to peak:

* squat
* bench
* deadlift

as if you have a powerlifting meet.

Strength matters.

Gym competition specificity does not.

## Common mistake #7: replacing Muay Thai conditioning with gym circuits

This program does not need to feel like a fight.

Keep your strength work:

* strength work.

Muay Thai provides the sport-specific suffering.

## Common mistake #8: ignoring Saturday sparring

Your gym program exists inside a week.

If Saturday is the session that matters most:

organize the rest of the week around it.

Do not blindly perform high-volume legs on Friday.

## Common mistake #9: treating the plan as permanent

Training needs change.

A block with:

* two easy Muay Thai classes

is not the same as:

* four hard sessions plus sparring.

Adjust volume.

## Common mistake #10: believing more exercises means more complete training

A program does not need to contain every movement you have ever seen online.

A few exercises performed consistently can cover a lot.

## FAQ

### Are two gym days enough for Muay Thai?

They can be.

Two full-body sessions are a practical structure for many people training Muay Thai several times per week.

It is not a universal optimum.

### Can I get stronger lifting twice per week?

Yes.

Progressive resistance training performed consistently can improve strength with two weekly sessions.

### Should both days be full body?

They do not have to be.

But full-body sessions make it easy to distribute training stress across two days.

### Should I use heavy weights?

Use loads appropriate to the rep range and your experience.

You do not need maximal lifts.

The main sets should be challenging while preserving good technique.

### How many reps should I leave in reserve?

For this program:

roughly:

**1–3 RIR**

for most strength work is a useful starting range.

This is a practical fatigue-management choice, not a magic physiological threshold.

### Should I train to failure?

Not routinely.

Failure is not required for strength gains and creates greater acute fatigue.

### Should fighters do low reps only?

No.

This program uses a mixture of:

* lower
* moderate

rep ranges.

Different loading ranges can develop useful adaptations.

### Should I perform power work before strength work?

Usually yes.

If the goal is maximum movement speed and quality, perform explosive work while fresh.

### Do I need box jumps?

No.

They are optional.

### Do I need medicine-ball throws?

No.

Useful if:

* available
* performed safely

but not required.

### Should I deadlift?

Only if the movement fits you.

An RDL, trap-bar deadlift, hip thrust, or another hinge can fill the role.

### Should I squat?

Again:

you need a useful lower-body strength movement.

You do not specifically need one squat variation.

### Are machines okay?

Yes.

Machines can build strength and muscle.

Use them when they fit the program.

### Can I use this plan with three Muay Thai sessions?

Yes.

That is one of the easiest schedules for it.

### Can I use it with four Muay Thai sessions?

Yes, but you may need lower gym volume.

Pay particular attention to:

* sparring
* leg fatigue
* total recovery.

### Can I lift and train Muay Thai on the same day?

Yes.

Control fatigue and decide which session is the priority.

### How long should I separate the sessions?

More recovery time can be useful when possible.

There is no universal required number of hours.

Several hours between sessions is a practical option when your schedule allows it.

### Should I lift before Muay Thai?

If strength is the priority for the day:

you can.

If Muay Thai skill quality is the priority:

doing Muay Thai fresh often makes more sense.

### What if my legs are sore all week?

Reduce:

* lower-body sets
* new exercises
* training to failure
* poorly timed leg work

before abandoning strength training entirely.

### Should I do extra cardio?

Only if it serves a specific conditioning goal.

Do not add cardio automatically just because the strength session did not exhaust you.

### How long should I run this program?

You can use the basic structure for:

**many months**

if it continues to work.

Exercises or loading can change gradually without rebuilding the entire program every few weeks.

### When should I change exercises?

Good reasons include:

* pain
* equipment limitations
* stalled progress
* changing goals
* disproportionate fatigue
* boredom that affects consistency

Do not change simply because:

**four weeks passed.**

### Should I deload?

Use a deload when your training plan and fatigue justify it.

It does not need to happen on a fixed calendar.

### What if I only have 30 minutes?

Use the priority exercises.

For Day A:

1. Lower-body lift
2. Push
3. Pull
4. Trunk if time

For Day B:

1. Power if appropriate
2. Hinge
3. Pull
4. Press

Skip optional work.

### Can beginners use this program?

Yes, but start with fewer sets and simple exercises.

Technique and consistency matter more than maximum volume.

## Final takeaway

A good two-day gym program for Muay Thai should be:

**simple enough to recover from**

and:

**hard enough to make you stronger.**

Day A gives you:

* lower-body strength
* pushing
* pulling
* single-leg work
* trunk strength

Day B adds:

* optional explosive work
* hip-hinge strength
* vertical pulling
* pressing
* support work

Keep most sets:

**1–3 reps short of failure.**

Progress:

* repetitions
* load
* movement quality

gradually.

Protect important:

* pad sessions
* sparring
* technical training

And when Muay Thai workload goes up:

**gym volume can come down.**

That is not losing progress.

That is making the strength program serve the sport it was supposed to support.
