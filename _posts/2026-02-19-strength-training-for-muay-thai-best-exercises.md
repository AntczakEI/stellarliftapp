Strength training for Muay Thai does not need to look complicated.

You do not need unstable circus exercises.

You do not need a bodybuilding programme with 25 hard sets for one muscle group.

And you do not need to turn every gym session into another conditioning workout.

The gym has a simpler job.

It should help you develop physical qualities that support Muay Thai while creating a level of fatigue you can actually recover from.

That usually means improving:

* lower-body strength
* hip and posterior-chain strength
* upper-body pulling and pressing strength
* trunk strength
* single-leg control
* explosive force production
* general physical robustness

The exact exercise matters less than whether you can perform it well, progress it over time, and fit it around your actual Muay Thai training.

This guide combines the most useful strength and power exercises into one practical list.

## What should strength training do for Muay Thai?

Muay Thai itself already trains:

* technique
* timing
* rhythm
* distance
* coordination
* repeated striking
* sport-specific conditioning
* tactical decision-making

The gym does not need to imitate all of that.

Strength training should fill gaps that are easier to develop with external resistance.

That includes:

* producing more force
* maintaining posture under load
* strengthening muscles around the hips, knees and shoulders
* improving general pulling and pushing strength
* developing a stronger trunk
* improving the ability to express force quickly

The goal is not to turn a squat into a fake kick.

The goal is to build useful physical qualities and then let Muay Thai practice turn those qualities into sport-specific skill.

## Does strength training actually help combat-sport athletes?

Research across combat sports supports the use of structured strength training.

A 2023 systematic review of strength-training interventions in Olympic combat-sport athletes found improvements in measures including maximal strength and muscular power.

The studies covered sports such as:

* boxing
* judo
* karate
* wrestling
* fencing

That does not mean every result transfers directly to Muay Thai.

Muay Thai has its own technical and physical demands.

But the evidence does support the broader idea that combat-sport athletes can benefit from organised strength training rather than avoiding weights because they are afraid of becoming slow.

Research on plyometric training in combat-sport and martial-arts athletes also suggests that well-programmed explosive work can improve measures of strength, jump performance and power.

The useful lesson is not that fighters need dozens of specialised exercises.

It is that **basic strength plus a small amount of high-quality explosive work is a reasonable combination**.

## What makes an exercise useful for Muay Thai?

A good gym exercise usually does at least one of these things:

* allows meaningful force production
* can be progressively overloaded
* trains several useful muscle groups
* improves a clear weak point
* has a manageable recovery cost
* can be performed consistently and safely
* does not require excessive technical practice unrelated to your sport

You do not need every exercise on this list.

A good programme selects a small number of them and repeats them long enough to make progress.

## 1. Squat variations

Squats are useful because they train the lower body through a large range of motion while requiring trunk control.

Useful options include:

* front squat
* back squat
* goblet squat
* safety-bar squat
* machine or belt-squat variations when appropriate

You do not need to argue about which one is universally best.

Choose a version that:

* you can perform comfortably
* fits your anatomy and training history
* can be progressed
* does not consistently ruin your Muay Thai sessions

### Front squat

The front squat can be a good option because it trains:

* quads
* glutes
* trunk
* upper-back position

A practical rep range might be:

**3 sets of 4–6 reps**

That is only an example.

The important point is to keep the volume manageable.

### Goblet squat

Goblet squats work well for:

* beginners
* lighter training days
* warm-up work
* higher-rep lower-body training

For example:

**2–3 sets of 8–12 reps**

Once the dumbbell becomes the limiting factor, another squat variation may become easier to progress.

## 2. Trap bar deadlift

The trap bar deadlift is a useful full-body strength exercise.

It trains:

* hips
* legs
* back
* grip
* trunk bracing

It can work particularly well in a simple full-body fighter programme because one exercise covers a lot of ground.

A common approach is:

**2–4 sets of 3–6 reps**

There is no reason to max out regularly.

Most of your training should consist of strong, controlled repetitions rather than grinders.

## 3. Romanian deadlift

Romanian deadlifts are one of the simplest ways to train the posterior chain.

They target:

* hamstrings
* glutes
* hip extension strength
* trunk control

This can complement the large amount of knee-dominant work that many fighters already get through:

* kicking
* footwork
* skipping
* squatting
* pad work

A practical starting point is:

**2–3 sets of 6–10 reps**

Control the lowering phase.

Do not turn the movement into a flexibility contest.

## 4. Split squats

Muay Thai constantly puts you in asymmetrical and single-leg positions.

That makes unilateral strength work useful.

Options include:

* Bulgarian split squats
* regular split squats
* reverse lunges
* step-ups

Benefits include:

* training each leg separately
* exposing obvious left-right differences
* developing hip and knee control
* building useful leg strength without requiring extremely heavy loading

The downside is soreness.

A large amount of unfamiliar split-squat volume can make kicking miserable for several days.

Start conservatively.

For example:

**2 sets of 6–10 reps per side**

Then build only if your recovery allows it.

For more detail, read [Should You Train Legs With Muay Thai?](/training/should-you-train-legs-with-muay-thai/).

## 5. Pull-ups and chin-ups

Fighters benefit from having a strong upper back.

Pull-ups and chin-ups train:

* lats
* upper back
* arms
* grip
* trunk control

They also give you a large compound pulling movement without requiring much equipment.

Use:

* bodyweight reps
* assisted reps
* weighted reps
* neutral-grip versions

depending on your level.

A reasonable range for many people is:

**3 sets of 5–10 reps**

If you cannot yet do full pull-ups, lat pulldowns are completely fine.

You do not need to pass a pull-up test before you are allowed to train your back.

For a deeper guide, read [Pull-Ups for Muay Thai](/training/muay-thai-pull-ups/).

## 6. Rows

Rows are another basic upper-back movement that fits almost every fighter programme.

Useful variations include:

* chest-supported row
* cable row
* machine row
* one-arm dumbbell row
* one-arm cable row

Rows can help build:

* upper-back strength
* pulling strength
* scapular control
* general shoulder support

They also balance weeks that contain large amounts of punching and pressing.

A simple prescription:

**2–4 sets of 6–12 reps**

Choose a version that lets you train the back rather than spending the whole set fighting your body position.

## 7. Pressing movements

Fighters do not need to avoid pressing.

They also do not need enormous pressing volume.

Useful options include:

* dumbbell bench press
* incline dumbbell press
* bench press
* overhead press
* landmine press
* push-up variations

The exact choice depends on:

* shoulder comfort
* equipment
* training history
* the rest of your programme

A practical approach is usually:

**2–3 sets of 5–10 reps**

Keep the total pressing volume sensible, especially if you already do several Muay Thai sessions every week.

Your shoulders are already working.

The gym should strengthen them, not constantly leave them exhausted.

## 8. Carries

Loaded carries are simple and useful.

Examples include:

* farmer carries
* suitcase carries
* front-loaded carries

They train combinations of:

* grip
* trunk
* posture
* upper back
* walking under load

They are also easy to understand.

Pick something up.

Maintain position.

Walk.

A simple setup:

**2–4 carries of around 20–40 metres**

You do not need to stagger around with a load so heavy that technique disappears.

## 9. Core training

Core work for fighters does not need to mean hundreds of crunches.

The trunk helps:

* transfer force
* resist unwanted movement
* maintain posture
* connect upper- and lower-body actions

Useful exercises include:

* dead bugs
* Pallof presses
* plank variations
* side planks
* carries
* hanging knee raises
* controlled rotational work

Recent research specifically in striking combat sports suggests that structured core-strength training may improve some technical-performance measures, although the evidence base is still relatively small.

The practical conclusion is simple:

Train the trunk.

Just do not pretend one special core exercise is going to magically create knockout power.

For more detail, read [Core Training for Fighters](/training/core-training-for-fighters/).

## Strength is only half of the picture

A strong fighter still needs to express force quickly.

That is where power work can help.

Power exercises should usually be performed:

* early in the gym session
* while you are relatively fresh
* with low-to-moderate volume
* with high movement quality

Once speed falls dramatically, the power portion of the session is basically over.

## 10. Jumps

Simple jump variations can develop explosive lower-body intent.

Options include:

* box jumps
* broad jumps
* countermovement jumps
* low-volume hurdle jumps for experienced athletes

You do not need dozens of repetitions.

A simple setup might be:

**3 sets of 3 jumps**

or:

**4 sets of 2 jumps**

Each rep should look explosive.

If the session turns into cardio, you have probably done too much.

Research on plyometric training in combat-sport athletes has reported improvements in several physical-performance measures, including strength and jump-related qualities.

That supports using plyometrics as a small part of training.

Not as the entire programme.

## 11. Medicine-ball throws

Medicine-ball throws are useful because you can move the load explosively without having to slow it down at the end of the movement.

Examples include:

* rotational wall throws
* scoop throws
* chest passes
* overhead throws

For fighters, rotational throws are particularly attractive because they allow powerful hip-and-trunk rotation.

But remember:

A medicine-ball throw is not striking practice.

It develops a general explosive quality.

Your pads, bag work and sparring still teach you how to apply force through an actual strike.

A simple dose:

**3–5 sets of 3–5 explosive reps**

Stop before speed clearly drops.

## 12. Explosive push-ups

Explosive push-ups can be a simple upper-body power exercise.

Options include:

* fast push-ups
* hands-leaving-floor push-ups
* clap push-ups for people who already have the strength and control

Keep repetitions low.

You want speed.

Not a high-rep chest workout.

## 13. Kettlebell swings

Kettlebell swings can train rapid hip extension when technique is good.

They can be used for:

* power-focused sets
* general posterior-chain training
* conditioning

The programming matters.

If you perform low-rep, high-quality swings with full recovery, they behave more like power work.

If you perform long sets while exhausted, they become conditioning.

Both can have a place.

Do not confuse the two.

## What about Olympic lifts?

Olympic lifts can develop power.

They are not mandatory.

Exercises such as:

* cleans
* snatches
* derivatives of Olympic lifts

require technical skill and appropriate coaching.

If you already know how to perform them well, they can fit.

If you do not, you are not missing a secret ingredient.

You can build strength and power with:

* squats
* deadlift variations
* jumps
* medicine-ball throws
* explosive push-ups

without spending months learning Olympic lifting.

Exercise selection should serve the athlete.

Not the other way around.

## What about conditioning exercises?

Conditioning is important in Muay Thai.

But this article is deliberately not turning into another giant conditioning guide.

Your sport already provides conditioning through:

* pad rounds
* bag work
* drilling
* clinch
* sparring

Additional conditioning can be useful when it targets a real weakness.

Useful options may include:

* assault bike
* rower
* running
* sled work
* skipping
* controlled intervals

But adding conditioning after every strength session because you want to feel exhausted can quickly interfere with recovery.

For a dedicated guide, read [Muay Thai Conditioning Exercises](/training/muay-thai-conditioning-exercises/).

## Sled pushes and drags

If you have access to a sled, it can be a useful tool.

Sled work allows you to train:

* leg drive
* work capacity
* general conditioning

without a lot of complex technique.

It can still be extremely fatiguing.

Use it as programmed conditioning.

Not automatically as a finisher after every gym session.

## What exercises should Muay Thai fighters skip?

There is almost no normal gym exercise that every Muay Thai athlete must permanently ban.

The better question is:

**Which exercises or programming choices produce more fatigue, pain or complexity than they are worth for you?**

Several common problems show up repeatedly.

### 1. Huge high-volume leg days

Muay Thai already places a large demand on the lower body.

A gym session containing:

* heavy squats
* deadlifts
* leg press
* lunges
* leg extensions
* leg curls
* calf work

all at high volume may be possible.

That does not mean it is useful.

If your leg session leaves your kicks and footwork poor for three days, reduce the dose.

The gym is supposed to support Muay Thai.

### 2. Repeated training to failure

You do not need every working set to end in failure.

Repeated failure training can create a larger fatigue cost than stopping slightly earlier.

For most main strength work, a useful default is to leave around:

**1–3 repetitions in reserve**

That is not a law.

It is simply a practical way to get productive work without turning every set into a maximal effort.

### 3. Excessive bodybuilding isolation

Isolation exercises are not automatically useless.

Useful examples can include:

* hamstring curls
* calf raises
* tibialis work
* lateral raises
* rotator-cuff work
* arm work
* hip work

The problem starts when accessory work consumes most of your recovery while the important movements receive little attention.

A fighter can do curls.

Curls just do not need to become the centre of the programme.

### 4. Weekly max attempts

Testing strength and building strength are different things.

If you attempt maximal singles constantly, you add fatigue and technical breakdown without necessarily improving faster.

Most fighters do not need frequent one-rep-max testing.

Use repeatable training loads.

Progress them over time.

### 5. Fancy instability exercises

Standing on unstable surfaces while pressing, squatting or throwing weights may look athletic.

But making an exercise unstable often reduces the amount of force you can produce.

Balance work can be trained directly when needed.

Strength exercises generally work better when you have enough stability to actually produce force.

Do not mistake complexity for specificity.

### 6. Exercises that hurt

Do not keep an exercise because somebody online called it essential.

If a movement consistently causes joint pain:

* check technique
* modify range
* change the variation
* reduce load
* replace it

There are usually several ways to train the same broad movement pattern.

You do not get bonus points for forcing one specific exercise.

## What about "fighter-specific" exercises?

The term is often abused.

A gym exercise does not become better for Muay Thai because:

* you stand in a fighting stance
* you wear gloves
* you add a resistance band to a punch
* the movement looks vaguely like a kick

Specificity comes primarily from Muay Thai training itself.

The gym can remain relatively general.

Build:

* strength
* power
* trunk capacity
* robust joints and tissues
* basic athletic qualities

Then practise Muay Thai.

That separation keeps both forms of training clearer.

## How many exercises do you need?

Not many.

A good full-body strength session might contain:

1. one explosive exercise
2. one main lower-body strength exercise
3. one hinge or secondary leg exercise
4. one upper-body push
5. one upper-body pull
6. one trunk or carry exercise

That is already a complete workout.

You may add an accessory if you have a specific need.

More exercises do not automatically create more progress.

## Example Strength Session A

### 1. Box jump

3 × 3

### 2. Front squat

3 × 4–6

### 3. Romanian deadlift

2–3 × 6–8

### 4. Dumbbell bench press

3 × 6–8

### 5. Pull-ups

3 × 5–10

### 6. Pallof press

2–3 sets per side

That is enough.

## Example Strength Session B

### 1. Medicine-ball rotational throw

4 × 3 per side

### 2. Trap bar deadlift

3 × 3–5

### 3. Split squat

2 × 6–10 per side

### 4. Overhead or incline press

2–3 × 5–8

### 5. Chest-supported row

3 × 8–12

### 6. Farmer carry

3 carries

Again, simple.

## How this fits into your Muay Thai week

Exercise selection is only half the problem.

Placement matters.

A good strength workout can still become a bad idea if you place it immediately before the Muay Thai session that matters most.

For example, if Saturday is hard sparring, Friday evening may not be the best time for:

* high-volume split squats
* hard deadlifts
* excessive leg accessories

You do not need a rigid rule that every gym session requires exactly 48 hours of recovery.

Instead, watch your actual performance.

If a strength session repeatedly ruins the next important Muay Thai session:

* reduce volume
* change exercise selection
* change the day
* lower effort
* remove unnecessary accessories

For the full weekly structure, read [Muay Thai Workout Plan: How to Build a Weekly Gym Program Without Ruining Your Fight Training](/training/muay-thai-workout-plan-weekly-gym-program/).

## How hard should you lift?

Strength training should feel like training.

It does not need to feel easy.

But the goal is repeatable work.

For most normal strength sets:

* use clean technique
* keep the rep speed reasonably controlled
* stop before ugly grinders become normal
* leave some repetitions in reserve

Power exercises are different.

For jumps and throws:

* stop when speed falls
* prioritise quality
* use complete enough rest to stay explosive

Do not perform power work like conditioning.

## How should you progress?

Use simple progressive overload.

You can increase:

* repetitions
* load
* sets when appropriate
* exercise difficulty

A simple double-progression method works well.

Example:

Target:

**3 sets of 5–8 reps**

You might progress:

* 3 × 5
* 3 × 6
* 3 × 7
* 3 × 8

Then add a small amount of weight and return toward the lower end of the range.

Progress does not need to happen every session.

Your Muay Thai workload changes.

Sleep changes.

Sparring changes.

Some weeks the best decision is simply to repeat the previous workload.

For more detail, read [Progressive Overload Explained](/training/progressive-overload-explained-how-to-keep-getting-stronger/).

## What if the gym keeps making you sore?

Soreness is not the goal.

If gym sessions repeatedly create enough DOMS to reduce:

* kicking quality
* footwork
* sparring
* technical training

look first at:

* too much volume
* too many new exercises
* too much eccentric loading
* frequent failure training
* poor placement in the week

Do not automatically remove strength training.

Reduce its recovery cost.

If you are unsure whether pain is normal soreness or something more concerning, read [DOMS vs Strain: How to Tell Muscle Soreness From a Possible Injury](/recovery/doms-vs-strain/).

## How often should Muay Thai athletes lift?

There is no universal number.

For many recreational and intermediate fighters:

**two full-body strength sessions per week**

is an excellent starting point.

One session may be enough when:

* Muay Thai volume is high
* you are in a particularly demanding training period
* strength maintenance is the main goal

Three sessions can work when:

* Muay Thai frequency is lower
* strength development is currently a priority
* recovery is good

More gym days are not automatically better.

The correct number is the one that gives you enough strength stimulus without consistently degrading the sport you actually care about.

## Does lifting make you slower?

Not automatically.

Strength training can improve force production and power.

What often makes fighters feel slow is fatigue.

Examples:

* excessive gym volume
* high soreness
* poor sleep
* too many hard days in a row

A well-managed programme should build useful physical qualities without leaving you permanently tired.

## Should fighters squat and deadlift?

They can.

Neither exercise is mandatory.

Squatting and hinging are useful movement patterns, but there are many ways to train them.

A fighter can use:

* front squats
* goblet squats
* trap bar deadlifts
* Romanian deadlifts
* split squats
* machines when appropriate

The programme matters more than loyalty to one lift.

## Do fighters need bench press?

No single pressing exercise is mandatory.

Bench press can be useful.

So can:

* dumbbell press
* incline press
* overhead press
* push-ups
* landmine press

Choose a version you can train productively without aggravating your shoulders.

## Do fighters need Olympic lifts?

No.

They can be useful when coached well.

But jumps and medicine-ball throws provide simpler power-training options for many recreational athletes.

## Do fighters need kettlebells?

No.

Kettlebells are tools.

They can be useful for:

* swings
* carries
* presses
* squats

But there is nothing uniquely "fighter-specific" about owning a kettlebell.

## Are machines bad for fighters?

No.

Machines can be useful for:

* adding muscle
* training around limitations
* reducing technical complexity
* accessory work
* targeting specific tissues

Free weights are not automatically athletic.

Machines are not automatically non-functional.

Use the tool that solves the problem.

## What matters more: exercise selection or programming?

Programming.

A good exercise can be programmed badly.

A basic exercise can produce excellent results when it is:

* progressed sensibly
* placed well in the week
* performed consistently
* used at an appropriate volume

Do not spend six months looking for the perfect fighter exercise while changing your programme every week.

## Common mistakes

### Trying to train every physical quality in one session

You do not need:

* heavy strength
* bodybuilding
* plyometrics
* sprinting
* conditioning
* mobility

all in one gym session.

Prioritise.

### Adding power exercises after exhausting strength work

Explosive exercises work best while you are fresh enough to be explosive.

Put them early.

### Doing too much leg volume

Strong legs help.

Destroyed legs do not.

### Chasing soreness

The goal is adaptation.

Not difficulty walking.

### Copying bodybuilding programmes

Hypertrophy work can be useful.

But a full bodybuilding split can become difficult to recover from when Muay Thai is also a priority.

### Copying professional fighters blindly

You do not know:

* their full training week
* what they actually do year-round
* their coaching support
* their recovery resources
* whether a social-media clip represents their real programme

Use principles, not random clips.

## Final takeaway

Good Muay Thai strength training is not complicated.

Build the programme around a small number of useful movements.

For most fighters, that means some combination of:

* squats
* hinges
* single-leg work
* pulling
* pressing
* carries
* trunk training
* jumps
* medicine-ball throws

Keep explosive work fast.

Keep strength work progressive.

Keep total volume low enough that your Muay Thai still looks like Muay Thai.

And skip exercises or programming choices that create a large recovery cost without solving a real problem.

The gym should make you a better-prepared fighter.

It should not become another sport you have to recover from.
