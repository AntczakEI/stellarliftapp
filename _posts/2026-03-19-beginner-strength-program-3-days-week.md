If you are new to lifting, you do not need:

* a six-day split
* 17 exercises per workout
* advanced periodization
* muscle confusion
* a new program every month

You need a few things done consistently:

* repeatable exercises
* enough resistance to create adaptation
* a progression method
* enough recovery to repeat the process

This program gives you exactly that.

It uses:

**three full-body sessions per week**

with a simple:

**A / B / C**

structure.

The goal is to help a beginner build:

* strength
* muscle
* exercise skill
* confidence in the gym

without making the program unnecessarily complicated.

## The short version

Train:

**3 days per week**

with at least one non-lifting day between most sessions when practical.

For example:

### Monday

Day A

### Wednesday

Day B

### Friday

Day C

Most exercises use:

**2–3 working sets**

and moderate repetition ranges.

Most working sets finish with roughly:

**1–3 repetitions in reserve.**

Progress primarily by:

**adding repetitions first, then adding weight.**

That is enough structure for a beginner to make substantial progress.

## Is three days per week optimal?

Not universally.

Resistance training can work with many different schedules.

You can become stronger training:

* twice per week
* three times per week
* more frequently

provided the total program is appropriate.

Three days works particularly well for beginners because it offers frequent practice without requiring you to spend most of the week in the gym.

## Why full body?

Again:

not because full-body training has some special anabolic advantage.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis comparing:

* split routines
* full-body routines

found similar effects on:

* strength
* muscle growth

when training volume was comparable.

So you can choose either structure.

For a beginner training three days per week, full body is simply convenient because you practise major movement patterns repeatedly across the week.

## Who this program is for

This plan is suitable if you:

* are new to resistance training
* have trained inconsistently and want a proper structure
* can train three times per week
* want a mixture of strength and muscle development
* want simple progression rules

You do not need to be completely untrained.

It can also work well if you are returning after:

* a long break

and want to rebuild gradually.

## Who may need something different?

You may need a more individual plan if:

* you are already an experienced lifter
* you have specific powerlifting goals
* bodybuilding is your main competitive goal
* you are rehabilitating an injury
* your available training time is extremely limited
* another sport dominates your weekly training load

If you are specifically trying to combine lifting with Muay Thai, use [Muay Thai Workout Plan: How to Build a Weekly Gym Program Without Ruining Your Fight Training](/training/muay-thai-workout-plan-weekly-gym-program/) rather than automatically adding this entire 3-day plan.

## The program at a glance

### Day A

1. Squat pattern
2. Horizontal push
3. Horizontal pull
4. Hip hinge accessory
5. Trunk or carry

### Day B

1. Hinge pattern
2. Vertical push
3. Vertical pull
4. Single-leg movement
5. Calves
6. Side trunk

### Day C

1. Squat variation
2. Push variation
3. Pull variation
4. Hamstring exercise
5. Optional arms

That gives you repeated exposure to:

* knee-dominant lower-body work
* hip-dominant lower-body work
* pushing
* pulling
* unilateral movement
* trunk training

without requiring a different exercise for every muscle.

## Day A

### 1. Squat pattern — 3 × 6–10

Choose one:

* Goblet Squat
* Front Squat
* Back Squat
* Leg Press

Use a load that lets you finish most sets with approximately:

**2–3 reps in reserve.**

Rest:

**2–4 minutes**

when the sets are challenging.

## Which squat should a beginner choose?

You do not need to start with:

**barbell back squats.**

A goblet squat may be easier to learn.

A leg press may be useful if you want:

* a stable lower-body exercise
* simpler loading

A front or back squat can be introduced when:

* technique
* confidence
* equipment

make them appropriate.

There is no prize for choosing the most complicated variation.

## 2. Horizontal push — 3 × 6–10

Choose one:

* Dumbbell Bench Press
* Barbell Bench Press
* Machine Chest Press
* Push-Ups

Keep:

**1–3 RIR**

on most sets.

Rest:

**2–3 minutes**

if needed.

## Dumbbells or barbell?

Either works.

Dumbbells may be convenient for beginners because:

* load can be modest
* each arm works independently
* setup can feel less intimidating

Barbell bench pressing is also perfectly valid.

Choose the version you can:

* perform comfortably
* repeat consistently
* progressively load

## 3. Horizontal pull — 3 × 8–12

Choose one:

* Seated Cable Row
* Chest-Supported Row
* Machine Row
* One-Arm Dumbbell Row

Use controlled repetitions.

You do not need to exaggerate:

* squeezing
* slow negatives
* strange tempos

to make a row effective.

Train the movement through a comfortable range and progressively load it.

## 4. Hip hinge accessory — 2 × 8–12

Choose one:

* Romanian Deadlift
* Hip Thrust
* Back Extension

If your RDL technique is still new:

start light.

The goal is to learn:

**hip movement under load**

not to see how much hamstring soreness you can create.

## 5. Trunk or carry — 2–3 sets

Choose one:

### Farmer Carry

**20–40 metres**

### Dead Bug

**6–10 reps per side**

### Pallof Press

**8–12 reps per side**

### Plank

**20–45 seconds**

You do not need several core exercises.

Pick one and perform it well.

---

## Day B

### 1. Hinge pattern — 3 × 5–8

Choose one:

* Trap-Bar Deadlift
* Romanian Deadlift
* Conventional Deadlift
* Hip Thrust

Do not automatically choose:

**conventional deadlift**

because it looks more serious.

For a beginner, a trap bar or RDL may sometimes be simpler to learn.

Use the exercise that fits:

* your technique
* equipment
* comfort

## Avoid duplicating Day A unnecessarily

If Day A already contains:

**Romanian deadlifts**

you probably do not need another heavy RDL on Day B.

You might use:

### Day A

Back Extension

and:

### Day B

Romanian Deadlift

or:

### Day A

Light RDL

and:

### Day B

Hip Thrust

The program is a movement template.

Not a requirement to repeat the same stress several times.

## 2. Vertical push — 3 × 6–10

Choose one:

* Dumbbell Overhead Press
* Barbell Overhead Press
* Machine Shoulder Press
* Landmine Press

Use a pain-free range.

You do not need one specific pressing variation for the program to work.

## 3. Vertical pull — 3 × 6–10

Choose one:

* Lat Pulldown
* Assisted Pull-Up
* Pull-Up

If you cannot yet perform clean pull-ups:

use assistance.

That is better than:

* kicking
* swinging
* performing half repetitions

simply to say you did pull-ups.

## 4. Single-leg movement — 2 × 8–12 per side

Choose one:

* Split Squat
* Reverse Lunge
* Step-Up
* Rear-Foot-Elevated Split Squat

Start conservatively.

Unfamiliar single-leg exercises can create considerable soreness.

You do not need:

**four sets per leg on your first week.**

## 5. Calf raise — 2 × 10–15

Choose:

* Standing Calf Raise
* Seated Calf Raise
* Leg-Press Calf Raise

Use controlled repetitions.

## 6. Side trunk — 2 sets

Choose:

### Side Plank

**20–45 seconds per side**

or:

### Suitcase Carry

**20–30 metres per side**

That is enough.

---

## Day C

Day C uses slightly different variations and generally moderate repetition ranges.

### 1. Squat variation — 3 × 8–12

Choose one:

* Leg Press
* Goblet Squat
* Hack Squat
* Light Front Squat
* Light Pause Squat

If Day A contains your heavier squat:

Day C does not need to be another maximal squat day.

Use a variation you can control.

## 2. Push variation — 3 × 8–12

Choose one:

* Incline Dumbbell Press
* Machine Chest Press
* Close-Grip Push-Up
* Dumbbell Bench Press

Assisted dips can also work if you already tolerate them comfortably.

They are not mandatory.

## 3. Pull variation — 3 × 8–12

Choose one:

* One-Arm Dumbbell Row
* Cable Row
* Machine Row
* Chest-Supported Row

If you used one row on Day A:

consider using another comfortable option here.

But you do not need constant variety.

Repeating the same row is also fine.

## 4. Hamstring exercise — 2 × 10–15

Choose one:

* Seated Leg Curl
* Lying Leg Curl
* Sliding Hamstring Curl
* Light Romanian Deadlift

If your week already contains substantial hinge work:

a leg curl is often a simple way to add knee-flexion hamstring work without another heavy hinge.

## 5. Optional arms — 2 × 10–15 each

### Curl

Choose any comfortable curl.

### Triceps

Choose:

* Pressdown
* Cable Extension
* Dumbbell Extension

This section is:

**optional.**

If you have limited time:

skip it.

Your program will not collapse because you missed curls.

## How hard should you train?

Most working sets should be:

**challenging but controlled.**

A useful starting point is approximately:

**1–3 repetitions in reserve.**

### 3 RIR

You probably had about three clean reps left.

### 2 RIR

Challenging but comfortable.

### 1 RIR

Hard set.

Probably one good repetition left.

These are estimates.

You do not need perfect accuracy.

## Do beginners need to train to failure?

No.

A meta-analysis comparing:

* failure
* non-failure

resistance training found that training to muscular failure was not required for strength or hypertrophy gains.

So you do not need to turn every set into:

**the last rep of your life.**

Stopping slightly short of failure makes it easier to:

* maintain technique
* control fatigue
* repeat the exercise later in the week

## Does that mean failure is dangerous?

No.

Failure training is not automatically dangerous.

And occasionally reaching failure on a:

* machine
* curl
* small accessory exercise

is not necessarily a problem.

The point is simply:

**it is not required.**

For a beginner learning technique, it is rarely necessary to make failure the default.

## What weight should you start with?

Start lighter than your ego wants.

Choose a weight where you can:

* reach the bottom of the rep range
* maintain good technique
* still have approximately 2–3 reps left

For example, if the range is:

**6–10 reps**

and you could perform:

**15**

easily:

the weight is probably too light.

If you barely manage:

**6**

with technique deteriorating:

it is probably too heavy for the purpose.

## Your first two weeks should feel manageable

A beginner does not need to discover their maximum immediately.

Weeks 1–2 can be used to:

* learn exercises
* establish starting loads
* practise logging
* learn what RIR feels like

You have plenty of time to increase difficulty.

## Progression: double progression

This remains the simplest progression method for this program.

Suppose an exercise uses:

**3 × 6–10.**

Keep the same weight and gradually increase repetitions.

Example:

### Session 1

8 / 7 / 7

### Session 2

9 / 8 / 7

### Session 3

9 / 9 / 8

### Session 4

10 / 10 / 9

### Session 5

10 / 10 / 10

Now:

**increase the load slightly.**

Your repetitions may fall back to:

* 7
* 7
* 6

That is normal.

Build up again.

For a deeper explanation, read [Progressive Overload Explained: How to Keep Getting Stronger Without Guessing](/training/progressive-overload-explained-how-to-keep-getting-stronger/).

## How much weight should you add?

Use the smallest practical increase.

For example:

### Upper body

Often:

**1–2.5 kg total**

if available.

### Lower body

Often:

**2.5–5 kg**

may be practical.

But the correct jump depends on:

* the exercise
* equipment
* your current strength

A beginner using small dumbbells may need a proportionally larger increase because the gym does not offer smaller jumps.

That is okay.

## You do not have to add weight every workout

Progressive overload does not mean:

**the bar must get heavier every Monday.**

Progress can include:

* more repetitions
* more weight
* better technique
* more stable repetitions
* larger comfortable range of motion

Beginners often progress quickly.

But progress is never perfectly linear forever.

## What if you cannot add reps?

One bad session means nothing.

Possible reasons include:

* poor sleep
* stress
* normal day-to-day variation
* inadequate food
* previous training
* simply having an off day

Repeat the same load next time.

Do not redesign the program because:

**Wednesday was bad.**

## What counts as a real stall?

A practical stall is when the same exercise fails to progress across:

**several exposures**

despite:

* reasonable sleep
* consistent technique
* adequate recovery

and performance may even be declining.

That is when you troubleshoot.

## Stall fix #1: repeat the load

The first solution is often:

**do nothing dramatic.**

Repeat it.

You may progress next time.

## Stall fix #2: reduce the load slightly

If the exercise has become:

* grindy
* technically poor

reduce the load by roughly:

**5–10%**

and rebuild.

This is sometimes called a:

**reset.**

It is not failure.

It is simply another way of creating progression runway.

## Stall fix #3: reduce volume

If you are struggling on many exercises at once and feel:

* unusually sore
* tired
* unmotivated

the problem may be accumulated fatigue.

Try temporarily reducing:

**one set from several exercises**

rather than pushing harder.

## Stall fix #4: change the exercise only when there is a reason

Good reasons include:

* pain
* equipment changes
* prolonged plateau
* poor fit
* loss of confidence with the exercise

Do not replace an exercise every time progression slows for:

**one week.**

Consistency lets you measure improvement.

## Should you add volume?

Not automatically.

The original program suggested adding sets during weeks 5–12 if recovery was good.

That can work.

But you do not need to increase volume simply because:

**week five arrived.**

Current resistance-training evidence supports the importance of training volume, particularly for hypertrophy.

But more is not automatically better indefinitely.

A beginner can make substantial progress with modest volume.

## Earn extra volume

Consider adding one set when:

* progress has been good
* soreness is manageable
* sessions are not excessively long
* recovery is good
* you want more muscle-growth stimulus

Add it to:

**one exercise or category at a time.**

For example:

### Day A Row

3 sets → 4 sets

Do not suddenly add:

* one set to squats
* one set to bench
* one set to rows
* another exercise

all in the same week.

## You may never need to add sets

If the existing program keeps producing:

* strength progress
* muscle gain
* good recovery

there is no requirement to make it larger.

A program does not improve simply because it becomes longer.

## Rest periods

The original version used:

* 90–180 seconds for major lifts
* 60–120 seconds for accessories

That is a reasonable starting point.

But do not turn those numbers into rigid limits.

For demanding compound exercises:

**2–4 minutes**

may be more appropriate.

For smaller exercises:

**1–2 minutes**

often works well.

## Rest until performance is ready

If you are still breathing hard and your next set falls apart because you started too early:

rest longer.

Short rest intervals can make a workout:

* harder

without necessarily making it:

* better.

A 2024 systematic review on hypertrophy found little evidence that very short rests have an advantage, with results tending to favour rests longer than 60 seconds.

## Do not turn lifting into cardio accidentally

Strength training does not need to feel like:

* circuit training
* HIIT

to be effective.

If your goal is strength:

allow enough rest to perform the next set well.

## Warm-up

You do not need the exact same warm-up forever.

A simple structure works.

### Step 1 — General movement

Approximately:

**3–5 minutes**

of something easy:

* walking
* bike
* rower

This is optional if you are already physically warm and active.

### Step 2 — A few relevant movements

Examples:

* bodyweight squats
* hip hinges
* shoulder movement
* light trunk work

### Step 3 — Exercise-specific warm-up sets

This is the most important part before a heavy first exercise.

Gradually increase the load.

## Example squat warm-up

Suppose your working weight is:

**60 kg.**

You might perform:

* bodyweight squats
* empty bar
* 30 kg
* 45 kg
* then working sets

You do not need to copy these exact numbers.

The point is:

**gradually approach your working load without tiring yourself out.**

## Warm-up sets are not working sets

Do not take warm-up sets close to failure.

They prepare you.

They are not supposed to exhaust you.

## Mobility before lifting

If you feel restricted in:

* ankles
* hips
* upper back

a few targeted movements can help you get comfortable before training.

You can use [10-Minute Mobility Routine: Daily Reset for Hips, Ankles, and Upper Back](/recovery/10-minute-mobility-routine/) on days when you want more mobility work.

But you do not need a mandatory 20-minute mobility ritual before every gym session.

## Exercise substitutions

The original plan was deliberately flexible.

Keep that.

You are allowed to replace an exercise without destroying the program.

Try to keep the same general movement role.

## Squat replacements

You can move between:

* Goblet Squat
* Front Squat
* Back Squat
* Leg Press
* Hack Squat

depending on:

* equipment
* comfort
* experience

## Hinge replacements

Options include:

* Romanian Deadlift
* Trap-Bar Deadlift
* Hip Thrust
* Back Extension

Conventional deadlifts are optional.

## Push replacements

Use:

* Dumbbell Bench Press
* Barbell Bench Press
* Machine Press
* Push-Ups

for horizontal pressing.

Use:

* Dumbbell Overhead Press
* Machine Shoulder Press
* Landmine Press

for more vertical or angled pressing.

## Pull replacements

Use:

* Cable Row
* Machine Row
* Dumbbell Row

for horizontal pulling.

Use:

* Lat Pulldown
* Assisted Pull-Up
* Pull-Up

for vertical pulling.

## Machines are fine

A beginner does not need a:

**free-weights-only rule.**

The 2026 ACSM evidence review found that equipment type did not consistently determine outcomes.

Machines can be especially useful because they are:

* stable
* simple to adjust
* easy to progress

Free weights are also useful.

You can use both.

## Do exercises need to be "functional"?

All training occurs in a context.

A leg press does not need to imitate:

* walking
* sport
* daily life

to strengthen your legs.

A row does not need to reproduce a real-world movement perfectly.

Exercises are tools for producing physical adaptations.

Your entire program creates transfer through:

* strength
* muscle
* movement skill
* overall physical capacity.

## Full range of motion

Use a comfortable and controlled range.

The 2026 ACSM position stand found an advantage for complete range of motion for strength development.

That does not mean:

**force every joint into the deepest possible position.**

Use the range that you can control and tolerate.

Over time:

you may improve that range.

## What if an exercise hurts?

Do not confuse:

* effort
* muscle fatigue

with:

* sharp joint pain
* unusual pain

If an exercise causes pain:

stop and assess.

You can often choose a different variation.

Persistent or significant pain deserves appropriate professional assessment.

## Muscle soreness is not a progress score

As a beginner, you may be sore when exercises are new.

That often reduces as your body becomes familiar with them.

You do not need to create:

**maximum DOMS**

after every workout.

Progress is better measured with:

* weight
* repetitions
* technique
* consistency

If you are unsure whether discomfort resembles normal soreness or something more concerning, use [DOMS vs Strain: The Simple Difference Between Muscle Soreness and Injury](/recovery/doms-vs-strain/).

## How long should you run the program?

The original version said:

**8–12 weeks.**

That remains a useful first block.

But nothing magical happens at:

* week 8
* week 12

If after 12 weeks:

* you are progressing
* exercises feel good
* you enjoy the plan

you can continue.

A program does not expire because the calendar says so.

## When should you change the program?

Change it when there is a reason.

For example:

* persistent plateau
* changing goals
* equipment changes
* exercise intolerance
* boredom affecting adherence
* different schedule

Do not change simply because:

**you need to shock the muscles.**

Your muscles do not require weekly surprises.

## How to track workouts

Record:

* exercise
* weight
* repetitions
* number of working sets

Optionally include:

* estimated RIR

Example:

**DB Bench — 22 kg — 10 / 9 / 8 — ~2 RIR**

That gives you enough information to make the next decision.

## You do not need complicated analytics

A beginner usually does not need:

* velocity trackers
* readiness algorithms
* spreadsheets with 40 variables

Simple records are enough.

Can you see:

* more reps?
* more load?
* cleaner movement?

Then the program is probably moving forward.

## Recovery between sessions

Progress happens because training creates a stimulus and you subsequently recover and adapt.

The basics matter:

* sleep
* adequate nutrition
* hydration
* manageable training volume

They matter more than most recovery gadgets.

For sleep, use [Sleep After Training: How to Recover Better Without Fancy Gadgets](/recovery/sleep-after-training-how-to-recover-faster-and-perform-better/).

For weekly recovery, use [How Many Rest Days Do You Really Need? Gym + Muay Thai Recovery](/recovery/how-many-rest-days-per-week-gym-muay-thai/).

## Do you need exactly 48 hours between sessions?

No.

Monday / Wednesday / Friday is convenient.

It is not a biological law.

You could train:

* Tuesday
* Thursday
* Saturday

just as easily.

Occasionally having two closer sessions is not automatically a problem either.

What matters is whether:

* performance
* soreness
* fatigue

remain manageable.

## Nutrition

You do not need a complicated bodybuilding diet to begin resistance training.

You do need enough overall nutrition to support:

* training
* recovery
* your goals

Protein intake matters for muscle adaptation.

But this article is a training program, not a diet plan.

Do not let nutritional optimization delay you from:

**actually training consistently.**

## Hydration

Normal hydration matters for training performance.

For most normal gym sessions:

you do not need an elaborate hydration protocol.

Drink according to:

* thirst
* conditions
* sweat loss
* session duration

For more detail, read [How Much Water to Drink Before, During and After a Workout](/hydration/how-much-water-before-during-after-workout/).

## Should beginners add cardio?

You can.

Cardiovascular exercise has its own health and fitness benefits.

A simple starting point might be:

**1–2 easy sessions per week**

or regular:

* walking
* cycling

outside the gym.

But do not assume cardio must be performed:

**after every lifting session.**

## Cardio should not destroy your recovery

If you add:

* hard intervals
* long runs
* lots of sport

your total training load increases.

Adjust accordingly.

## What if you also train Muay Thai?

This program is not specifically designed around combat sports.

If you train Muay Thai:

**3–5 times per week**

adding the entire program may create more total workload than you need.

Options include:

* reducing gym frequency
* cutting sets
* moving to the dedicated 2-day fighter program

Use [Best 2-Day Full Body Gym Workout for Muay Thai Fighters](/training/muay-thai-strength-training-program-2-days-week/) if Muay Thai is your main priority.

## What if you miss one workout?

Do not panic.

If you miss Wednesday:

you can simply continue with:

**Day B**

at your next available session.

You do not need to:

* double workouts
* cram six days into five
* restart the whole program

Consistency is measured across months.

Not one imperfect week.

## What if you miss an entire week?

Resume with slightly conservative loads if needed.

You probably do not need to:

* restart from zero

after one week away.

If you have been away significantly longer:

reduce load and volume while you readapt.

## Do you need a deload?

Not on a fixed schedule.

A beginner may go months without needing a formal deload.

Consider reducing training when you see a cluster of signs such as:

* several workouts getting worse
* unusual persistent fatigue
* worsening soreness
* poor motivation
* difficulty recovering

One bad day is not a deload signal.

## Simple deload option

Keep the same exercises.

For approximately one week:

* reduce working sets by around one-third to one-half
* keep loads manageable
* stay further from failure

Then resume normal training.

For a deeper explanation, read [Deload Week for Muay Thai + Gym: When to Do It and How to Do It](/recovery/deload-week-muay-thai-gym/).

## Common mistake #1: adding too much

The program gives you:

* five or six movements per session

That is enough.

Do not immediately add:

* chest flyes
* three biceps exercises
* leg extensions
* another deadlift
* 20 minutes of abs
* a finisher

Run the actual program first.

## Common mistake #2: chasing failure

A set does not need to end with:

* shaking
* screaming
* forced repetitions

to stimulate adaptation.

Hard, controlled sets are enough.

## Common mistake #3: changing exercises every week

If your exercises constantly change:

you cannot easily tell whether you are progressing.

Keep the main movements stable for several weeks.

## Common mistake #4: adding weight before earning it

If the target is:

**6–10 reps**

and you are still achieving:

**6 / 5 / 4**

with poor technique:

you do not need more weight.

Build performance at the current load first.

## Common mistake #5: using terrible technique just to log progression

Adding:

**5 kg**

does not represent useful progression if:

* range becomes much shorter
* control disappears
* another person effectively performs the rep for you

Make comparisons reasonably consistent.

## Common mistake #6: assuming sweat means a better workout

Resistance training does not need to leave you:

* soaked
* breathless

to work.

Your goal is not to maximize sweat.

## Common mistake #7: treating every session like a test

Training builds strength.

Constantly testing:

* 1RM
* maximum reps

is not the same as building it.

Beginners benefit from accumulating:

**good training sessions.**

## Common mistake #8: ignoring recovery

If every session gets worse because you:

* sleep poorly
* train every day
* add extra sports
* keep increasing volume

the solution may not be:

**more motivation.**

You may simply need less total stress.

## Common mistake #9: thinking soreness proves growth

You can grow without severe soreness.

You can also become very sore without building an effective long-term program.

Use measurable progression instead.

## Common mistake #10: program hopping

You run this for:

**two weeks.**

Then another program appears online.

Two weeks later:

you change again.

Now nothing stays consistent long enough to evaluate.

Give a reasonable program time.

## FAQ

### Is three days per week enough to build strength?

Yes.

Three resistance-training sessions per week can provide more than enough opportunity for a beginner to gain strength.

### Can I build muscle on this program?

Yes.

The program provides resistance-training volume across the major muscle groups.

Muscle growth will also depend on:

* effort
* progression
* nutrition
* recovery
* individual response.

### Is full body better than a split?

Not inherently.

Research comparing split and full-body routines shows similar results when volume is matched.

Full body is simply convenient for this three-day beginner structure.

### Do I need to train exactly Monday, Wednesday and Friday?

No.

Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday works equally well.

Use three days that fit your life.

### Can I train three days in a row?

Occasionally, perhaps.

But for this beginner program, spacing sessions is usually more comfortable and easier to recover from.

### How long should workouts take?

Usually around:

**45–70 minutes.**

Do not rush major sets simply to meet a stopwatch.

### How many exercises should I do?

Approximately:

**4–6 per session**

in this program.

You do not need to add more initially.

### How many sets do beginners need?

There is no universal exact number.

This plan starts mostly with:

**2–3 working sets per exercise**

which is a practical beginner dose.

### Should I train to failure?

Not routinely.

It is not required for strength or hypertrophy gains.

### How many reps in reserve?

Most sets can finish around:

**1–3 RIR.**

Beginners may initially stay closer to:

**2–3 RIR**

while learning technique.

### Should I lift heavy?

Some exercises use lower rep ranges and relatively heavier loads.

But you do not need:

* maximum weights
* constant 1RM attempts.

### Is 5 reps better for strength than 10?

Heavier loading tends to have advantages for maximal strength development.

But beginners can gain substantial strength across a range of loads.

That is why this program uses several rep ranges.

### Is 8–12 reps the hypertrophy range?

It is a useful range.

It is not the only range capable of producing muscle growth.

### Do I need barbell squats?

No.

### Do I need conventional deadlifts?

No.

### Can I use machines?

Yes.

### Are machines worse for beginners?

No.

They can be very useful because they provide stable and repeatable resistance.

### Should I do cardio?

You can.

Keep total training load appropriate.

### Can I use this with Muay Thai?

Possibly, if your Muay Thai volume is low.

If combat sports are a major part of your week, a lower-volume strength plan is usually easier to manage.

### What if I am sore?

Mild general soreness may be normal.

Reduce or modify training if soreness substantially changes movement.

Be more cautious with:

* sharp
* sudden
* localised

pain.

### When should I add weight?

When you reach the top of the target repetition range with:

* good technique
* appropriate RIR

across the required sets.

### What if my gym only has large weight jumps?

Add repetitions first.

If the next weight jump is too large:

you may spend longer at the current weight.

### What if I stall?

First:

* repeat the load

Then consider:

* a small reset
* less volume
* more recovery

before replacing the entire program.

### Do I need a deload every month?

No.

Deload when your training and fatigue justify it.

### How long can I keep this program?

As long as it:

* fits your goals
* remains tolerable
* continues producing progress

You do not need to replace it after an arbitrary number of weeks.

## Final takeaway

A beginner strength program should be:

**simple enough to repeat**

and:

**structured enough to measure.**

Train three full-body sessions each week.

Use:

* a squat pattern
* a hinge
* pushes
* pulls
* some unilateral work
* basic trunk training

Keep most sets:

**1–3 reps short of failure.**

Add repetitions.

Then add load.

Track what you actually do.

Do not keep changing the program simply because something new appeared online.

The goal is not to make your training look complicated.

The goal is to become:

**measurably stronger over time.**
